# Harvester-ant nest architecture, excavation behaviour and spoil handling (Messor structor first; Messor / Veromessor / Pogonomyrmex as comparison)

Scope note for the report writer: I found **no accessible peer-reviewed field excavation or cast of a *Messor structor* nest**. Every M. structor number below is from Wikipedia (flagged "citation needed"), hobbyist care sheets, or Russian forum answers, and is marked as weak. The most detailed and reliable quantitative data come from Walter Tschinkel's casts of the Florida harvester ant *Pogonomyrmex badius* (a different genus, similar ecology: seed harvester, sandy soil, workers ~same size class), plus older French field work on the Saharan *Messor arenarius*, and lab digging experiments on *Messor sancta* (= *M. structor*-group relative used by Buhl/Deneubourg/Theraulaz), *Lasius niger*, *Formica pallidefulva* and *Solenopsis invicta*. Genus/species is flagged on every finding.

## Q1. Messor structor nest: depth, chambers, granaries, brood, entrances, change with colony size and season

### Takeaway
There is no measured architecture for *M. structor* in sources I could reach; weak sources describe a deep (likely ≥1 m, "several metres" claimed) nest of one or a few vertical/inclined shafts with side chambers, a crater-shaped entrance ringed by seed husks, often under stones, colonies of ~1,000–5,000 workers, and dry deep granaries with brood in moister zones. The best Messor-genus proxy (*M. arenarius*, Sahara) has nests ~2–3 m deep with granaries at ~2 m, small 1-cm-high chambers, inclined corridors and 1–5 crater entrances metres apart.

### Cited Findings
**Messor structor (weak sources only)**
- Nests in the ground, "usually under larger stones", with "several larger chambers" — [AntSite.eu (hobbyist)](https://antsite.eu/en/ants/messor-structor/)
- Wild nests have "crater-like entrances surrounded by seed husks" (care guide, no field data) — [AntScout](https://antscout.com/species/messor-structor)
- Russian Q&A answer: soil nests can reach "several metres", usually consist of 2–3 sections (a vertical channel with side chambers, connected by passages); entrance is a crater ~5–10 cm across. User-generated, not peer reviewed — [otvet.mail.ru](https://otvet.mail.ru/question/15957558)
- Russian keeper site: underground part is a long vertical tunnel with lateral passages and chambers; above ground an opening surrounded by an earthen rampart ("вал из земли") — [muravdom.ru](https://muravdom.ru/help/kartochki-vidov/messor-structor-stepnye-muravi-zhnecy-soderzhanie-i-uxod-v-domashnix-usloviyax/)
- Recommended chamber height in captivity 9–12 mm (keeper wiki) — [wiki.muravdom.ru](https://wiki.muravdom.ru/ants/myrmicinae/messor-structor/); manufacturer formicaria use 9–15 mm — [4ants.ru](https://4ants.ru/product/muravinaya-ferma-antrum-m-derevo-messor-structor-2)
- Seed stores "1–2 kg or more" in underground chambers [citation needed]; workers remove seed appendages before storage (Pacini 1990); colony size "up to ~5,000 workers in the wild" [citation needed]; workers ~4–10 mm, strongly polymorphic; Austrian populations unicolonial (no clear nest boundaries, no aggression between nests, no distinct mating flights; Schlick-Steiner et al. 2006) — [Wikipedia: Messor structor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messor_structor)
- Colony size estimates conflict: ~1,000–2,000 (AntScout), up to ~5,000 (Wikipedia), up to 50,000 (fourmishome.fr, outlier) — [AntScout](https://antscout.com/species/messor-structor); [fourmishome.fr](https://www.fourmishome.fr/Messor-structor-bbaaaacHa.asp)
- Care guides describe dry granaries and a more humid brood/queen zone (keeper practice, not field measurement) — [ant-shack.com](https://www.ant-shack.com/blogs/ant-care-sheets-1/messor-structor-the-steppe-harvester-ant)
- Overwintering requirements conflict between vendors (no hibernation vs. Nov–Mar at 5–8 °C); no field study found — search summary of [antontop.com](https://antontop.com/messor-structor-steppe-ants-home-care/) and other care sheets

**Messor arenarius (Sahara/Negev) — best Messor-genus field proxy.** All from a 2025 review by an independent researcher in *Open Journal of Ecology* (SCIRP, a low-tier venue) summarising older primary sources — [Warburg 2025, Open J. Ecol. 15:260–267](https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperinformation?paperid=141376)
- Nest area ~50–100 m², depth up to ~2 m (Forel 1894); possibly hundreds of m² with entrances ~10 m apart (Lameere 1902)
- Crescent-shaped sand craters up to 50 cm diameter (Lameere 1902); entrances are craters or half-crescents of sand balls, 1–5 exit holes per nest, 2–3 m apart, opened each evening (Délye 1968)
- Corridors diagonal (slightly inclined) to 60–80 cm, tracked to ~3 m; vertical corridors rare; rooms rare at depth (Délye 1968)
- One nest ~1,500 ants; corridors can exceed 10 m in length; small rooms ~3–5 cm wide, ~1 cm high (Délye 1971)
- Granaries at ~2 m depth; largest ~40 cm long × 10 cm wide × 2 cm high; vertical distance between deepest and highest granary ≤25 cm; larvae lie on "mattresses" of grain inside granaries (Délye 1971)
- Corridors and rooms roughly doubled as the colony grew (Délye 1971)
- Average ~20 m between neighbouring entrances in the Negev Highlands (Warburg & Steinberger 1997); some entrance corridors follow major roots of *Retama raetam*

**Veromessor pergandei (N. American desert, closely related to Messor)**
- Tschinkel's cast shows shafts that are "distinctly angled… expressed to an extreme degree in the desert dwelling *Veromessor pergandei*" — [Tschinkel 2015, J. Bioecon. 17:271–291](https://www.bio.fsu.edu/~tschink/publications/2015-beauty.pdf)
- An incipient nest (small ~65 mm outside-diameter crater at a road edge) held 9 queens, 40 nanitic workers and brood at 15 cm depth (Wheeler & Rissing 1975, Pan-Pac. Entomol. 51:205–216) — search summary of [BHL](https://biodiversitylibrary.org/part/241353)
- A young colony recognised by its "single, small crater and nest entrance" — [Pollock & Rissing 1985, Psyche 92:125](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/mac/projects/psyche/92/92-125.html)

**Pogonomyrmex badius (for depth zoning, since Messor lacks data)**
- Mature colonies commonly 2.5–3.0 m deep (max 3.06 m); incipient nests 29–50 cm; seeds stored only in chambers 40–100 cm deep; brood primarily in the bottom third; usually one entrance, sometimes two or three — [Tschinkel 2004, J. Insect Sci. 4:21](https://academic.oup.com/jinsectscience/article/4/1/21/886534)
- "Brood are found primarily in the bottom third or so of the nest, seeds in a zone above that but still well below the surface, and foragers only in the top 10–15 cm" — [Tschinkel 2015](https://www.bio.fsu.edu/~tschink/publications/2015-beauty.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a 64 × 36 cm slice at 1 px = 1 mm, a **mature** harvester-ant nest (1–3 m deep in every related species with data) will not fit; the slice shows only the top-heavy upper zone (foragers, superficial chambers, upper shaft). An **incipient/young colony** (tens to a few hundred workers; P. badius incipient nests 29–50 cm, V. pergandei founding chamber at 15 cm) fits the slice. Either model a young colony, scroll/extend the slice downward, or treat the bottom of the slice as "continues deeper".
- Plausible M. structor-like layout for the sim (synthesised from Messor/Veromessor/Pogonomyrmex): one main inclined/helical shaft ~1 cm wide (M. structor workers 4–10 mm), flat chambers ~1 cm high (Messor arenarius rooms ~1 cm high, P. badius ~1 cm, keeper chambers 9–15 mm), chambers widely spaced deeper down, granaries in a dry middle-deep zone, brood at the bottom, foragers near the surface.
- Unicolonial/polydomous behaviour reported for Austrian M. structor plus M. arenarius' multiple entrances metres apart suggest a sim could legitimately spawn a second entrance/shaft at some distance once the colony is large.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed measurement found for M. structor nest depth, chamber number, granary depth/size, entrance number, or crater dimensions. Russian (e.g. Dlussky's desert-ant works), Ukrainian, Hungarian and Turkish primary literature was not reachable via search; AntWiki pages returned 403 (Cloudflare) and could not be read.
- The "1–2 kg seed store" and "5,000 workers" figures are uncited on Wikipedia.
- No source found on seasonal change of M. structor entrances (e.g. closing/plugging in winter, reopening in spring).

## Q2. Spoil handling: crater, disc, mound, scattering, erosion, vegetation clearing, chaff middens

### Takeaway
Real harvester ants do **not** build an ever-growing tall pile: spoil is dropped in a low crater ring or a flat disc around the entrance (in P. badius a disc averaging ~1,500 cm² for a ~3 L nest, i.e. only ~2 cm thick on average), the disc is flattened and spread by wind/rain within about a year, and colonies periodically relocate (P. badius ~once per year, ~4 m away) so old spoil is abandoned. Some *Pogonomyrmex* (P. occidentalis) do keep a gravel-capped mound that houses upper chambers; chaff/husk middens are placed at the edge of the crater or disc, not on the entrance.

### Cited Findings
**Florida harvester ant, Pogonomyrmex badius (best quantitative spoil data)**
- Each relocation excavates a new nest as large as or larger than the old one in 4–6 days, depositing on average 4.8 kg of sand (max ~20 kg); 0.1–12 L of soil per colony per year on the surface (mean ~3 L); sand assumed 1.5 kg/L — [Tschinkel, Rink & Kwapich 2015, PLOS ONE "Biomantling and bioturbation…"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4368782/)
- Disc area 80 to ~5,700 cm², mean ~1,540 cm²; log–log regression of nest volume on disc area: intercept 0.098, slope 1.12, R² = 92%; max depth (cm) = 71.3 + 0.049 × disc area (cm²), R² = 60%; estimated nest volumes ~100–12,000 cm³ — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4368782/)
- Erosion/spread experiment: 20-cm discs of 500 g pink sand spread to >50 cm diameter within a year, grains found up to 1 m away; after two years detectable only under UV light, diluted from 100% to ~20% marker; mobile surface layer 2–5 mm thick; nest "ghosts" remain visible a year or more — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4368782/)
- Origin of spoil by depth (V = nest volume, cm³): 0–50 cm layer = −301 + 0.717V; 50–100 = −140 + 0.161V; 100–150 = 0.0573V; 150–200 = −84.7 + 0.0518V; 200–250 = −41.3 + 0.0329V; i.e. most spoil comes from the top 50 cm — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4368782/)
- Over a millennium one colony territory receives ~2,660 L, covering ~21% of the area, equivalent to a 0.43 cm uniform layer; 16 colonies/ha → ~64 t/ha per millennium; mounding rate 0.011 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹ — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4368782/)
- Subsurface deposition (backfilling) is a small fraction, mostly in the upper 30 cm; for *P. occidentalis* lab work suggested ~half of excavated soil ends up below ground, which the author considers unverified for natural colonies — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4368782/)
- About 2.5% of excavated sediment was deposited below ground (P. badius; Tschinkel, Rink & Kwapich 2015) — search summary of [PLOS ONE / DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/5f9dbf7abc2a456a8ccc5e10788a9250)
- "Soil excavated from deeper levels is dumped around the entrance, and superficial chambers often lie within that spoil" — [Tschinkel 2004](https://academic.oup.com/jinsectscience/article/4/1/21/886534)
- Nests marked by a disc of excavated soil decorated with charcoal bits (mostly burned pine-needle ends) — [Tschinkel 2013, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0059911)
- Soil is moved up by a chain: a pellet is removed, moved upward, deposited and picked up again by different workers until finally deposited on the surface (Rink et al. 2013) — [Tschinkel 2015](https://www.bio.fsu.edu/~tschink/publications/2015-beauty.pdf)
- Relocation: about once a year (range once per 2 years to 4 times a year), 840 moves recorded 2012–2013, mostly June–October; mean distance ~4 m (simulation used normal, mean 3.9 m, SD 3.15 m), a few >10 m, random direction — [Tschinkel et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4368782/); one move of 40 m; moving takes 4–7 days — [Tschinkel 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0059911)

**Red harvester ant, Pogonomyrmex barbatus (Arizona)**
- Mound ~1 m wide; mean mound area 2.27–2.82 m²; 1–3 surface entrances (older colonies ≥5 yr mean 1.7, younger ≤4 yr mean 1.3); all entrance tunnels lead to one entrance chamber (mean 7.3 × 3.7 cm, 2–3 cm high, 3–8 cm deep, mean 5.7 cm) — [Gordon 2025, Ecol. Evol. (PMC12413562)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12413562/)
- Entrance tunnel ~1.4 cm wide (SD 0.4), 7.9 cm long, sloping ~2 cm down per 3 cm horizontal; openings from ~1 cm holes to flat 2–3 cm openings above a runway — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12413562/)
- Nest maintenance workers carry soil and refuse out and deposit it on the mound or midden; they clear debris from blocked entrances after flooding; refuse and seed husks pile up in chambers adjacent to the entrance chamber and are removed at the end of the foraging period — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12413562/)
- Wind erosion in dry conditions produced craters ~10 cm deep × 20 cm wide in 40 of 120 exposed nests (2019); six collapsed nests with 2–8 exposed chambers were not repaired within 12 days but were fully repaired by the next year; collapse did not significantly affect survival; drought cracked ≥40 mound surfaces in 2023 — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12413562/)
- Floods push ants into lower "retreat" chambers; some colonies stay inactive under mud until a few dry days pass — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12413562/)
- "Nest discs" 60 cm to 4 m in diameter, granaries below the central entrance (McCook 1879, via review) — [Warburg 2025](https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperinformation?paperid=141376)
- Soil compaction significantly reduced toward disc centre; larger nests affect a larger soil area (Texas) — [Southwestern Entomologist 49(3)](https://bioone.org/journals/southwestern-entomologist/volume-49/issue-3/059.049.0310/Red-Harvester-Ant-Pogonomyrmex-barbatus1-Nests-Impact-Soil-Compaction-But/10.3958/059.049.0310.short)

**Western harvester ant, Pogonomyrmex occidentalis (true mound builder)**
- Mound made of excavated soil plus fine gravel collected from the surface; gravel probably protects against erosion by strong winds and torrential rain (function debated; may also mark the entrance); cleared area averages ~1.5 m diameter, up to 3 m in North Dakota; workers clear living and dead vegetation — search summary of [AntWiki: P. occidentalis](https://antwiki.org/wiki/Pogonomyrmex_occidentalis) (page itself returned 403; not directly verified)
- Mound irregularly conical, can exceed 89 cm in diameter; nest up to 5 m deep — [Wikipedia: P. occidentalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pogonomyrmex_occidentalis); contradicted on depth by Lavigne 1969 (Wyoming): max depth 2.7–2.8 m, many chambers at 30–46 cm, nurseries in large chambers directly below the mound — via [Warburg 2025](https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperinformation?paperid=141376)
- Vegetated rims around cleared discs receive extra nutrients and less competition — [Entomology Today 2024](https://entomologytoday.org/2024/01/25/harvester-ant-nest-rims-native-nonnative-plants-invasion-ecology-restoration/pogonomyrmex-occidentalis/)

**Messor / Veromessor spoil and chaff**
- M. arenarius: sand balls (bearing mandible prints) are carried out and deposited around entrances, where wind disperses them; entrances are craters or half-crescents of sand balls up to 50 cm diameter (Délye 1968, 1971; Lameere 1902) — [Warburg 2025](https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperinformation?paperid=141376)
- Classic observations of Messor: the refuse heap is always at the periphery of the crater or cleared earthen disc; rejected seeds sprout there and can form an arc or complete circle of plants around the nest — search summary of [Hölldobler & Wilson, The Ants, ch. 18 (AntWiki)](https://antwiki.org/wiki/The_Ants_Chapter_18) (page returned 403; snippet only)
- V. pergandei: each entrance surrounded by a crater of sand and fine gravel, with a neat semicircular chaff pile of seed husks on one side — search-result snippet only; primary source not verified
- Messor barbarus discards husks in a trash pile and stores larger seeds; refuse piles locally concentrate seed density and richness — [Azcárate et al. 2005, Funct. Ecol.](https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.0269-8463.2005.00956.x) (abstract-level)
- Husks and seed waste are disposed of in superficial chambers and around the nest (Messor waste dumps host the beetle *Oochrotus unicolor*) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/338141032_Strategies_of_the_beetle_Oochrotus_unicolor_Tenebrionidae_thriving_in_the_waste_dumps_of_seed-harvesting_Messor_ants_Formicidae)
- Messor ebeninus accumulates far more chaff per hectare than M. rugosus or M. arenarius (Negev) — [Steinberger et al., Chaff piles of Messor nests](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/chaff-piles-of-harvester-ant-messor-spp-nests-in-a-desert-ecosyst/)
- M. cephalotes colonies clear large oval bare patches around entrances of ~1.5–47 m² — [Wikipedia: M. cephalotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messor_cephalotes) (secondary)
- Messor barbarus nest density in Spanish cereal fields averages 953/ha (230–2,570/ha) — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0929139311001946); 284–900 nests/ha in NE Spain — [IOBC-WPRS](https://iobc-wprs.org/product/density-dependent-seed-removal-in-dryland-cereals-by-harvester-ants/)

**Construction pheromone (mechanism for why piles can self-amplify)**
- In *Lasius niger* building, deposition is amplified where fresh material carries a pheromone; pheromone lifetime was the most influential parameter for the form of the structure; ants use body size as a template for roof height on pillars — [Khuong et al. 2016, PNAS 113:1303](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4747701)

### Inferences
- The sim's "tall mound on both sides of the entrance that keeps growing" contradicts all harvester-ant data found. Realistic rules: (1) drop spoil at a distance from the entrance sampled over a ring/disc (the crater rim), not at the hole; (2) cap slope with an angle-of-repose-style slumping rule so the ring stays low; (3) add a slow erosion/diffusion pass (rain and wind) that spreads the disc outwards (P. badius discs >50 cm wide in a year, grains to 1 m) and flattens it; (4) let some spoil "leave the slice" to represent radial spreading in 3D; (5) optionally relocate the colony periodically (P. badius ~1/yr, ~4 m), abandoning the old disc.
- Mean P. badius disc thickness is roughly nest volume ÷ disc area ≈ 3,000 cm³ ÷ 1,540 cm² ≈ 2 cm (my arithmetic from Tschinkel et al. 2015 means) — i.e. a 20 px-thick, ~440 px-wide low lens at 1 px = 1 mm, not a tall cone.
- Conservation of mass in a 2D slice exaggerates mound height: in 3D, spoil from a central hole is spread over a disc whose area grows with r², so a 2D cross-section should only receive a fraction of excavated volume (a modelling choice, not a measured value).
- Chaff/husk middens should be a separate material placed at the outer edge of the crater/disc (and/or in shallow chambers near the entrance before being carried out), possibly on one side (V. pergandei semicircle claim).
- P. barbatus shows the opposite failure mode too: wind can scour a 10 × 20 cm crater into the mound and collapse surface chambers; repair takes weeks to a season. Exposed chambers after erosion are a realistic event.

### Gaps
- No quantitative data for M. structor crater diameter, rim height, disc size, or midden position (only "crater-shaped entrance ringed with husks").
- No measured rim height or slope for harvester-ant craters; angle of repose of dry sand (~30–35°) is general physics not sourced in this research.
- How rain specifically reshapes Messor craters (and whether Messor plug entrances during rain/winter) was not found.
- The V. pergandei "semicircular chaff pile" description and the Hölldobler & Wilson midden statement came from search snippets; full text not verified.

## Q3. Tschinkel's casts and related work: chamber area vs depth, volume vs workers, vertical distribution, relocation

### Takeaway
Harvester-ant nests (P. badius) are top-heavy shish-kebabs: a helical ~1 cm shaft with flat ~1 cm-high chambers, about half of chamber area in the top quarter, chamber spacing growing from 2–4 cm near the surface to 20–30 cm deep, and total chamber area scaling almost linearly with worker number (area ∝ N^0.87, depth ∝ N^0.37). The shape is size-invariant as the colony grows; colonies relocate about yearly and rebuild the same architecture in under a week.

### Cited Findings
All P. badius findings below are from [Tschinkel 2004, J. Insect Sci. 4:21](https://academic.oup.com/jinsectscience/article/4/1/21/886534) unless stated.
- Sample: 33 nests, 2–3.02 m deep, 5–150 chambers; one cast had 135 chambers and 12 m of shafts
- Max depth vs dark (adult) workers DW: log MaxDep(cm) = 0.95 + 0.37 log DW (R² = 72%); 10× more workers → ~2.4× deeper
- Total chamber area: log TotalArea(cm²) = 0.55 + 0.87 log DW (R² = 93%); 10× workers → ~7.5× area; volume follows the same pattern
- Chamber height ~1 cm regardless of area; average chamber near the surface 5–6× larger than near the bottom
- Area by depth: each depth decile holds 25–40% less area than the one above; about half of total area in the top quarter; log–log slope of decile area on depth −0.59
- Superficial chambers (<10–15 cm deep): mean 26 cm² in smallest colonies, ~330 cm² in the largest; formed by enlargement and branching of horizontal tunnels (perimeter often 2–7× that of a circle of equal area); deep chambers start round/oval and become lobed as enlarged — also [Tschinkel 2015](https://www.bio.fsu.edu/~tschink/publications/2015-beauty.pdf)
- Top-decile chamber area by colony size class (0: 0–100 workers; 1: 101–800; 2: 801–2,000; 3: 2,001–4,000; 4: >4,000): 38, 190, ~515, 1,700, 2,670 cm²
- Vertical chamber spacing 2–4 cm near the surface, 20–30 cm deeper, maximum at 70–80% of max depth
- Shafts: mean slightly under 1 cm diameter, upper shafts flattened ovals up to ~2 cm wide; helix diameter 4–6 cm (more often left-handed); descent angle ~15–30° near the surface rising to 45–70° below ~50 cm; loop length 8–10 cm near surface, ~20 cm deeper
- Branching mostly in the top half, rarely >2 branches per shaft; branch points at 12–17, 26–33 and ~35 cm, all shallower than 40 cm; 1 vertical "series" (shaft + chambers) in small nests, up to 4 (once 5) in large; mean workers by number of series ≈ 1,700 (1), 3,200 (2), 4,600 (3), 5,000 (4)
- Growth: simultaneous deepening, adding chambers/shafts and enlarging chambers, so relative distribution of area with relative depth does not change with colony size
- Workers: density increases with depth; young workers at the bottom, older higher; brood 20–40 per brood chamber in the top half, 60–110 in the bottom half; seeds only at 40–100 cm (citing Tschinkel 1999)
- CO₂ rises ~5-fold from surface to deepest chambers
- Total sand removed per nest: ~40 g (incipient) to ~40 kg (largest)
- In other species too, nest volume tracks colony size and the proportions of all elements stay constant during growth; larger ants need wider shafts and higher ceilings; natural fire-ant chambers average ~5 cm², limiting work groups to ~200 ants — [Tschinkel 2015](https://www.bio.fsu.edu/~tschink/publications/2015-beauty.pdf)
- Formica pallidefulva (different subfamily): nests 30–45 cm deep, top-heavy (volume declines exponentially with depth, coefficient −1.3 ± 0.46); most excavated volume in the top ~15 cm; log volume = 0.37 + 0.96 log workers (R² = 0.87; slope not different from 1 → volume ∝ workers); depth not correlated with worker number (possibly limited by water table); nests grow by adding whole new shafts — [Mikheyev & Tschinkel 2004, Insectes Soc. 51:30–36](https://www.bio.fsu.edu/~tschink/publications/2004-1.pdf)
- Relocation: P. badius moves ~once per year; old and new nests do not differ in chamber-area distribution, chamber shape, worker age or brood distribution; vented or reversed CO₂ gradients did not change architecture, so CO₂ is not the depth cue — [Tschinkel 2013, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0059911)
- P. barbatus (Arizona): dense chamber cluster to 20–30 cm in mature colonies, sparser chambers to ~1 m, nest depth roughly equal to mound width (~1 m), retreat tunnel to ≥2 m; chamber widths 0.6–7.8 cm (mean 4.5); inter-chamber tunnels <1 cm — [Gordon 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12413562/)
- P. occidentalis (lab, silty clay, glass-sided): galleries ~0.9–1.5 cm diameter, chambers ~3–10 cm long, up to 1.5–2 cm high — search summary of [Searchanddiscovery abstract (Halfen 2009)](https://www.searchanddiscovery.com/abstracts/html/2009/annual/abstracts/halfen.htm)

### Inferences
- Useful worked values from the P. badius regressions (my arithmetic): 100 workers → max depth ≈ 49 cm, total chamber area ≈ 196 cm²; 1,000 workers → ≈115 cm, ≈1,450 cm²; 5,000 workers → ≈207 cm, ≈5,900 cm². With ~1 cm ceilings, chamber volume ≈ 1.2–2 cm³ per worker. A colony of ~100–300 workers is the largest whose whole nest fits in a 36 cm-deep slice.
- For the 36 cm slice of a larger colony: expect the large, irregular, branched superficial chambers in the top 10–15 cm, closely spaced chambers (2–4 cm apart) in the top ~30 cm, shaft branch points at ~12–17 and ~26–33 cm, and the shaft leaving the bottom of the slice at a steepening angle.
- In a 2D side view, a helical shaft appears as a zig-zag/inclined path (shallow ~15–30° near surface, steeper deeper), not a straight vertical line.

### Gaps
- No cast or excavation of any *Messor* (Old World) species with chamber-area-vs-depth data was found; V. pergandei is figured by Tschinkel but numbers were not in the text read.
- P. badius queen location not stated in the parts read.

## Q4. Self-organised digging: stigmergy, templates, digging rates, stopping rules, branching, soil moisture and type

### Takeaway
Digging is self-organised: a pheromone-marked digging front recruits more digging (positive feedback, exponential phase), and digging stops when excavated volume reaches a value roughly proportional to the number of ants (negative feedback via falling ant density/aggregation and the ants' own changed state), restarting if ants are added. Measured per-ant rates are a few mm³ per ant per hour in the lab (Messor sancta ≈ 6–10 mg sand ant⁻¹ h⁻¹). Soil needs to be moist and cohesive: ants cannot dig in completely dry or saturated sand, and tunnels are deepest/most stable at intermediate moisture.

### Cited Findings
**Messor sancta (Buhl, Deneubourg, Grimal & Theraulaz)**
- Setup: moist sand disc 20 cm diameter × 5 mm thick under glass; 50, 100, 200 ants; 3-day runs. Digging dynamics logistic (exponential growth then saturation and stop). Final volume / dug mass rate / network length / number of galleries: 50 ants 25.25 cm³, 10.4 mg ant⁻¹ h⁻¹, 79.8 cm, 23.6; 100 ants 37.49 cm³, 7.7 mg ant⁻¹ h⁻¹, 115.6 cm, 40.2; 200 ants 60.9 cm³, 6.4 mg ant⁻¹ h⁻¹, 199.3 cm, 85.4 — [Buhl et al. 2001, Actes Coll. Insectes Sociaux 14:52–105](https://dictionnaire-amoureux-des-fourmis.fr/Noms%20propres/Publis/2001/ACTES-COLLOQUES-INSECTES-SOCIAUX-VOL14-Buhlf.pdf)
- Individual probabilities (constant over time): drop a sand pellet P_D = 0.188 s⁻¹; leave the sand-disc edge P_Q = 0.0176 s⁻¹; pickup/dig probabilities 0.102 and 0.054 per contact and 2.83×10⁻² s⁻¹ (table alignment in the scanned PDF is ambiguous about which value belongs to which action); digging probability with pheromone P_c = X²/(X² + K²), K = 200 where a crevice exists vs K = 3,500 without; diggers mark the site with pheromone and pellet-carriers lay a gradient trail; only with these markings did the model reproduce network growth; aggregation reached 85.3% (SD 12.6) at the end; digging stops when network volume reaches a critical value proportional to population — [same](https://dictionnaire-amoureux-des-fourmis.fr/Noms%20propres/Publis/2001/ACTES-COLLOQUES-INSECTES-SOCIAUX-VOL14-Buhlf.pdf)
- 50–200 workers: excavation began exponentially and after ~3 days reached saturation with activity almost stopped; final excavated volume positively correlated with ant number — [Buhl et al. 2004, Naturwissenschaften 91:602–606](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00114-004-0577-x) (abstract via search)
- Nest-size regulation can emerge from recruitment and density without a target size — [Buhl et al. 2005, Behav. Ecol. Sociobiol. 58:9–17](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00265-004-0906-2) (abstract via search)
- Tunnel branching rules (M. sancta, 50/100/200 ants): new tunnels start at the periphery, laterally from existing tunnels, or at nodes; branches leave roughly at right angles with Gaussian noise; a front hitting a tunnel creates a node, hitting a node enlarges it; active diggers decline at a constant rate; meshedness (loopiness) increases with group size and plateaus above ~2,000 simulated ants — [Gautrais et al. 2014, PLOS ONE "The role of colony size on tunnel branching morphogenesis"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4198125/)

**Lasius niger (Rasse & Deneubourg; Toffin et al.)**
- Final nest volume and digging rate grow almost proportionally with ant number; when ants are added (even slightly) the nest is enlarged again in the same way; the negative control is due to nest volume itself plus physiological/behavioural change in ants after digging — [Rasse & Deneubourg 2001, J. Insect Behav. 14:433–449](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1011163804217)
- 2D digging (2-mm layer of wet sand, 15% water, 90 h): final area A_M = 20.18 ± 7.99 cm² (50 ants) and 75.18 ± 42.76 cm² (300 ants), i.e. 0.40 vs 0.25 cm² per ant; time to half final area β ≈ 12.2 h (50) vs 8.8 h (300); circular cavity first (perimeter exponent ω ≈ 0.5), then branching into galleries at ≈60% of final area (A_Tra ≈ 0.60·A_M + 5.06); no transition in 40% of 50-ant vs 12% of 300-ant runs; transition at activity density ρ ≈ 0.04 h⁻¹ independent of group size — [Toffin et al. 2009, PNAS 106:18616](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2773997/)
- Toffin agent model: 600 × 600 lattice, cell ≈ 0.07 mm², ant = 4 cells wide, 1 step = 1 min; a cell is diggable if ≥1 of 8 neighbours is dug and reachable; dig probability depends on pheromone in neighbours (K = 50 units for 50% response, intrinsic ξ = 0.1%, each dug cell deposits 100 units, 5% evaporates per step); nonlinear (cooperative) digging reproduces the circle→branch transition, constant-rate digging does not — [same](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2773997/)
- The two morphological transitions occur more frequently in a cohesive substrate than in a granular one — [Toffin, Kindekens & Deneubourg 2010, Proc. R. Soc. B (PMC2982035; abstract-level via search, full text not read)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2982035)

**Solenopsis invicta (Monaenkova et al. 2015)**
- 100 ± 10 workers (~3.5 mm, 3.2 mg) in glass-particle substrates: "clay-like" d = 0.025 mm, "fine" 0.24 mm, "coarse" 0.7 mm; moisture W = 0–0.2 (saturation 0.22–0.28). Nests built in all except completely dry or fully saturated substrates; nest volume relatively insensitive to moisture at 0.05 < W ≤ 0.2; tunnels deepest at intermediate moisture; at low moisture tunnel depth was 2–3× lower than the maximum; depth correlated with substrate yield force (cohesion), interpreted as stronger nests "less prone to collapse"; nest growth rate increased with substrate coarseness; excavation rates 0.05–0.3 cm³ h⁻¹ per 100 ants in 3D and ~0.4 cm³ h⁻¹ for ~150 ants in 2D; tunnel diameter 3–5 mm (~one body length) regardless of substrate; mean pellet mass 0.37–0.47 mg, similar across substrates — [Monaenkova et al. 2015, J. Exp. Biol. 218:1295–1305](https://cbid.gatech.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Monaenkova-2015.pdf)
- Tunnel depth at moderate moisture (W = 0.1) was at least twice that at the lowest moisture in all 9 colonies — [APS March Meeting 2013 abstract](https://meetings-archive.aps.org/mar/2013/m29/11)

**Formica pallidefulva (Mikheyev & Tschinkel 2004)**
- Load 2.26 ± 0.18 mg sand per trip (≈1.56 mm³; paper prints "ml", evidently µl) = ~30% of 7.54 mg body mass; climbing speed 3.1 cm/s; loading time ~15 s; excavation increased with soil temperature and moisture; no effect of 0.4% CO₂; rules: chambers are dug in the direction of the tunnel leading to them; whole nest = 10,000–460,000 ant-hours (median 81,000) at 28 °C, ≈158 ± 66 h per worker, ~1 week regardless of colony size — [Mikheyev & Tschinkel 2004](https://www.bio.fsu.edu/~tschink/publications/2004-1.pdf)

**Pogonomyrmex badius field rates**
- ~5,000 workers (15–20 g) excavated ~20 kg of sand in 4–5 days; a whole nest takes 3–6 days regardless of colony size; per worker-day ≈0.45 cm² chamber area + 0.13 cm shaft (older workers) vs 0.15 cm² + 0.06 cm (young); 300–400× own body weight of sand per worker per day; older workers dig most (82% of old workers recaptured at the surface) — [Tschinkel 2004](https://academic.oup.com/jinsectscience/article/4/1/21/886534)

**Templates and cues**
- CO₂ was proposed as a depth cue but experiments (venting, reversing the gradient) showed it is not used — [Tschinkel 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0059911); [Tschinkel 2015](https://www.bio.fsu.edu/~tschink/publications/2015-beauty.pdf)
- Leaf-cutting ants (*Acromyrmex lundi*): new chambers are not dug in advance; they form where relocated brood/fungus aggregate workers; digging is higher and cavities rounder at brood sites (density-dependent digging); brood is moved away from low temperature/low humidity first — [Römer & Roces 2014, PLOS ONE](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4022738/)
- Ants move brood up and down daily to track favourable temperature/humidity; fire-ant heat-collecting mounds give ~10% brood-production benefit — [Tschinkel 2015](https://www.bio.fsu.edu/~tschink/publications/2015-beauty.pdf)

### Inferences
- Messor sancta rates convert (assuming moist sand bulk density ~1.5 g/cm³, as Tschinkel uses) to ≈4–7 mm³ ant⁻¹ h⁻¹; per-capita final volume falls with group size (≈0.50, 0.37, 0.30 cm³ per ant for 50, 100, 200 ants), i.e. final volume is somewhat sub-linear in that dataset even though the authors describe it as proportional.
- In a 2D lattice of 1 mm cells, the Messor sancta 5-mm-thick disc results correspond to ≈60–100 mm² (60–100 px) of excavated area per worker at saturation; Lasius niger 2-mm layers give 25–40 mm² per ant. A simple sim stop rule: target area ≈ k × N_workers (k ~ 25–100 px per worker in lab sand; scale up for real nests with brood/seed storage), digging probability falling as excavated area approaches target, resuming when N grows.
- P. badius field digging (~590 mm³ worker⁻¹ day⁻¹ ≈ 25 mm³ h⁻¹ if continuous; my arithmetic) is much faster than lab rates, probably because field nests are built by motivated relocating colonies with large workers; a sim could use a "relocation burst" mode vs a slow "growth" mode.
- For soil types: treat dry loose sand as undiggable/collapsing, moist sand-loam as ideal, saturated soil as undiggable; deeper stable tunnels require cohesion. Clay-like fine substrates dig slower than coarse ones (fire ant data).
- Use a pheromone/recruitment field at the digging front (positive feedback) plus a density or volume-per-ant brake (negative feedback) to get the observed exponential-then-saturating curve and the circle-to-branch transition.

### Gaps
- No direct digging-rate measurement for *M. structor*.
- Quantitative effect of humidity gradients as a digging template, and the 2025 bioRxiv study on temperature and excavation speed, were not read.
- Exact tunnel width, branching rates and digging rate values for the Gautrais et al. 2014 Messor sancta model are only in equation images/tables not extracted.
- No quantified collapse rule (e.g. maximum unsupported chamber span vs soil type) was found.

## Q5. Measured relationships most directly usable as simulation rules

### Takeaway
A defensible rule set can be built from: volume ∝ workers (Rasse & Deneubourg; Mikheyev & Tschinkel; P. badius area ∝ N^0.87), depth ∝ N^0.37, top-heavy chamber area declining ~25–40% per depth decile, ~1 cm chamber ceilings and ~1 cm shafts, chamber spacing growing with depth, logistic digging with pheromone positive feedback, per-ant pellet transport of ~0.4–2.3 mg per trip, and surface spoil spread into a low disc that erodes and is abandoned on ~yearly relocation.

### Cited Findings
- Max depth: log MaxDep(cm) = 0.95 + 0.37 log N; total chamber area: log A(cm²) = 0.55 + 0.87 log N (P. badius) — [Tschinkel 2004](https://academic.oup.com/jinsectscience/article/4/1/21/886534)
- Nest volume ∝ workers (slope 0.96, n.s. from 1; F. pallidefulva) — [Mikheyev & Tschinkel 2004](https://www.bio.fsu.edu/~tschink/publications/2004-1.pdf); nest volume and digging rate almost proportional to population, renewed digging when ants added (L. niger) — [Rasse & Deneubourg 2001](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1011163804217)
- Logistic dynamics with saturation in ~3 days (M. sancta) and half-final-area at ~9–12 h (L. niger, 2D) — [Buhl et al. 2001](https://dictionnaire-amoureux-des-fourmis.fr/Noms%20propres/Publis/2001/ACTES-COLLOQUES-INSECTES-SOCIAUX-VOL14-Buhlf.pdf); [Toffin et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2773997/)
- Pheromone-response digging: P = X²/(X²+K²) (M. sancta) — [Buhl et al. 2001](https://dictionnaire-amoureux-des-fourmis.fr/Noms%20propres/Publis/2001/ACTES-COLLOQUES-INSECTES-SOCIAUX-VOL14-Buhlf.pdf); lattice rule set with K = 50, ξ = 0.1%, deposit 100, evaporation 5%/step (L. niger) — [Toffin et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2773997/)
- Spoil deposited on surface: 0.1–12 L per colony-year (mean 3 L), disc 80–5,700 cm² (mean 1,540 cm²), disc spreads >50 cm/year, mobile layer 2–5 mm, ~2.5% backfilled below ground, relocation ~1/yr, mean 4 m — [Tschinkel et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4368782/)
- Vertical zoning: foragers top 10–15 cm, seeds middle (40–100 cm in P. badius), brood bottom third; workers migrate upward with age — [Tschinkel 2015](https://www.bio.fsu.edu/~tschink/publications/2015-beauty.pdf); [Tschinkel 2004](https://academic.oup.com/jinsectscience/article/4/1/21/886534)
- Messor-genus granaries flat (≤2 cm high, up to 40 × 10 cm) and clustered within ~25 cm vertical span (M. arenarius) — [Warburg 2025 citing Délye 1971](https://www.scirp.org/journal/paperinformation?paperid=141376)

### Inferences
- Suggested "realistic spoil" rule chain for the designer (synthesis): pellet carried up the shaft (possibly relayed between workers) → dropped on the surface at a distance sampled from ~2–25 cm of the entrance (crater rim; M. arenarius craters up to 50 cm, M. structor reportedly 5–10 cm) → slump if local slope exceeds a threshold → periodic rain/wind diffusion that lowers and widens the disc → optional removal of a fraction of spoil to represent out-of-plane spreading → husks to a separate midden at the disc edge → after a long time or when the disc gets too big, colony relocates a few body-lengths-of-slice away (or off-screen).
- To keep the slice uncluttered at 64 cm wide: a young Messor colony's crater (~5–10 cm) and disc (~10–45 cm) fit comfortably; spoil height should stay in the order of 1–3 cm (10–30 px) rather than a tall cone.

### Gaps
- All rule parameters are from P. badius, L. niger, M. sancta, F. pallidefulva and S. invicta; none are calibrated for M. structor.
- No measured distribution of drop distances from the entrance for any harvester ant was found.
