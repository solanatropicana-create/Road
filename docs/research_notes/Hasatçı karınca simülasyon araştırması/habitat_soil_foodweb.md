# Messor structor: habitats, soils, and the living world above and below ground (for a 2D soil cross-section simulation)

Scope note for the report writer: "Messor structor" was treated as one species across Europe and western Asia for most of the 20th century. Steiner et al. (2018) split it into five cryptic species with largely separate ranges. **M. structor in the strict sense is a Central European and northern Balkan species.** The Anatolian, Ukrainian, Russian and Caucasian "structor" populations belong to sister species (M. ponticus, M. muticus, M. mcarthuri). Ecologically they are near-identical steppe harvester ants, so they are fair models for an Anatolian or Pontic site, but the naming caveat should be stated in the game's flavour text or docs. Nest architecture is covered in a separate note (`nest_architecture_excavation.md`) and is only touched on here.

## 1. Where Messor structor (s.l.) lives: habitats, soils, exposure, altitude and climate across its range

### Takeaway
The Messor "structor" group lives in hot, dry, open, well-drained ground: dry and steppe grasslands, sand steppes, loess slopes, rocky steppe hills, pastures, roadsides and abandoned fields. The range runs from France and the Pannonian Basin east through the Balkans, the Black Sea steppes and Anatolia to the Caucasus and Central Asia. Rainfall across that range is roughly 250–650 mm/yr, winters are cold and continental, summers are hot, and sites lie from lowland up to about 1,450 m (Greece) and 750–1,250 m on the Anatolian plateau. In Central Europe it sits at its northern limit and is confined to the very warmest dry grasslands.

### Cited Findings
**Taxonomy and range (important for site naming)**
- Steiner et al. (2018) used morphometrics, Wolbachia, mtDNA, AFLP and ecological niche modelling. They split Messor "structor" into five species: M. structor, M. ibericus, M. muticus (stat. rev.), and two new species, M. ponticus and M. mcarthuri — [Steiner et al. 2018, Mol. Phylogenet. Evol. 127:387–404](https://www.antvid.org/PDF/Steiner_et_al._-_2018_-_Molecular_Phylogenetics_and_Evolution.pdf)
- Distributions given in that paper (Fig. 2, Table S1) — [Steiner et al. 2018](https://www.antvid.org/PDF/Steiner_et_al._-_2018_-_Molecular_Phylogenetics_and_Evolution.pdf):
  - **M. structor s.str.**: Austria, Bulgaria, Czech Republic, France, Hungary, Romania, Slovenia (neotype from Brive-la-Gaillarde, France).
  - **M. ibericus**: Bulgaria, Croatia, France, Germany, Greece, Italy, Romania, Slovenia, Spain, Switzerland.
  - **M. ponticus**: Bulgaria, Romania, Turkey, Ukraine. Named for the Black Sea region.
  - **M. muticus**: Armenia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Romania, Russian Federation, Ukraine.
  - **M. mcarthuri**: Greece, Turkey. Type locality is Köyceğiz, Muğla, Turkey.
- The niche models were built from 19 bioclimatic, 8 soil (SoilGrids) and 5 vegetation-cover variables. PC1 "roughly corresponded to soil and temperature" and PC2 to "seasonality and precipitation". Niche overlap between the species was low (Schoener's D 0.02–0.41), so **each species has a measurably different climate/soil niche** — [Steiner et al. 2018](https://www.antvid.org/PDF/Steiner_et_al._-_2018_-_Molecular_Phylogenetics_and_Evolution.pdf)
- M. structor and M. muticus are "well separated longitudinally… and ecologically" — [Steiner et al. 2018](https://www.antvid.org/PDF/Steiner_et_al._-_2018_-_Molecular_Phylogenetics_and_Evolution.pdf)
- Life history differs between populations. M. structor s.str. at **Retz (Lower Austria, a Weinviertel wine town)** was reported as unicolonial with no swarming flights. M. ibericus at Mainz (Germany) was reported as multicolonial with swarming flights — [Steiner et al. 2018](https://www.antvid.org/PDF/Steiner_et_al._-_2018_-_Molecular_Phylogenetics_and_Evolution.pdf); [Schlick-Steiner et al. 2006, Eur. J. Entomol. 103:469–476](https://www.eje.cz/pdfs/eje/2006/02/25.pdf)
- A Russian regional checklist argues that Steiner et al. sampled southeastern European Russia poorly (a single Krasnodar locality). The Lower Volga steppe populations' identity therefore remains uncertain — [rusant checklist page (search snippet)](https://rusant-lv.myspecies.info/taxonomy/term/54)
- Before the split, M. structor was thought to range over "North Africa and the Middle East, Southern, Central, and Eastern Europe, Asia Minor, the Caucasus, and Central Asia" — [Schlick-Steiner et al. 2006](https://www.eje.cz/pdfs/eje/2006/02/25.pdf)

**Habitat descriptions**
- In Lower Austria the species lives only in "near-natural, hot-dry dry grasslands" (naturnahe, heiß-trockene Trockenrasen), a habitat that is strongly fragmented there — [BOKU FWF project page](https://forschung.boku.ac.at/fis/suchen.projekt_uebersicht?sprache_in=de&menue_id_in=300&id_in=5010)
- In Central Europe it occurs only in "extremely warm dry grasslands": from the Middle Rhine to the Kaiserstuhl (now M. ibericus), and again in eastern Austria, neighbouring Slovakia and Hungary — [Spektrum Lexikon der Biologie, "Ernteameisen" (search snippet)](https://www.spektrum.de/lexikon/biologie/ernteameisen/22376)
- Czech record: M. cf. structor nests were found "on steppe, under stone" on Svatý kopeček hill near Mikulov (South Moravia, a limestone steppe hill) in May 2001 and May 2006 — [Růžička & Vávra 2012, Klapalekiana 48:190](https://coleoptera.fzp.czu.cz/pdf/Ruzicka&Vavra2012Klapalekiana_Leiodidae_Attaephilus_Moravia.pdf)
- Greek studies report the group from pastures, roadsides, stony hills, ruderal places in villages, and open pine and oak forests up to about 1,450 m. M. ponticus is a lowland species in Greece, nesting in pine forests and on roadsides among other places — [AntWiki pages for M. ponticus / Salata & Borowiec 2019, via search snippet](https://antwiki.org/wiki/Messor_ponticus)
- Keeper and retailer sources (not peer-reviewed) say it prefers sandy soils but is "relatively flexible" and also nests under stones or in earthier soils. A German hobby wiki says that in grassland it builds vegetation-covered mound nests up to 35 cm high. Treat both as low-confidence — [antcube.shop](https://antcube.shop/en/product/messor-structor); [Ameisenwiki (search snippet)](https://ameisenwiki.de/index.php/Messor_structor)
- The genus Messor "occurs in the New and Old World, most commonly in arid and semi-arid areas, grasslands, and savannahs" — [Plowes et al. 2013, Myrmecol. News 18:33–49](https://myrmecologicalnews.org/cms/index.php?option=com_download&view=download&filename=volume18%2Fmn18_33-49_printable.pdf&format=raw)

**Climate figures for representative regions**
- **Pannonian lowland (Hortobágy, E Hungary, loess grassland patches):** mean annual temperature 9.5 °C, precipitation 550 mm with high between-year variation. The rainfall maximum is typically in June (mean 80 mm) — [Tóth & Hüse 2014, Appl. Ecol. Environ. Res. 12:537–547](https://www.aloki.hu/pdf/1202_537547.pdf)
- **Vienna Basin / Marchfeld (E Austria, Pannonian):** mean annual temperature >9 °C, 500–600 mm, "high temperature and low precipitation in summer". Soils are Chernozem, Parachernozem and Wet Chernozem — [Predatory mites in grassy arable fallows in Eastern Austria, PMC3365242](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3365242/)
- **Central Anatolia:** the plain mostly lies at 750–1,250 m, with volcanic peaks above 2,000 m. The climate is semi-arid with cold winters and warm, dry summers, 300–650 mm/yr, and a mean annual temperature of 7–13 °C — [Central Anatolian steppe grazing model, PMC11532325](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11532325/)
- Konya gets "barely 12 inches" (~300 mm) a year, among the driest places in Turkey. Ankara averages −2 °C in January and 23 °C in July. Soils of the semi-arid central zone are brown to reddish-brown and mostly calcareous — [Britannica, Turkey: the central massif](https://www.britannica.com/place/Turkey/The-central-massif)
- Most of Central Anatolia gets <400 mm, and more than half falls in winter and spring. Steppe there sits on calcareous, marly, marly-gypsaceous and gypsaceous parent rocks. Floristic composition changes as mosaics over short distances because the parent rock changes quickly — [Synoptic view of Central Anatolian steppe vegetation, Asian J. Plant Sci. 2006 (search snippet; page 403 on fetch)](https://scialert.net/fulltext/?doi=ajps.2006.733.739)
- **Southern Ukrainian steppe (Askania-Nova, Kherson):** ~300–450 mm/yr, mean annual temperature +9.4 °C, January −1.5 to −5 °C, July 23–24 °C. The reserve protects Festuca–Stipa grassland on dark chestnut soils — [Askania-Nova ANPP study, Ekológia (Bratislava) 2017 (search summary)](https://reference-global.com/article/10.1515/eko-2017-0020)

### Inferences
- For game sites, the species label could be "M. structor" for Pannonian, Austrian, Czech and Romanian sites, and "M. structor-group (M. ponticus / M. mcarthuri)" for Anatolian or Black Sea sites. The ant can behave the same way in both; the label is a credibility detail.
- **Candidate starting sites, synthesised from the findings in sections 1–6:**
  1. **Pannonian loess steppe** (Hortobágy edge / Weinviertel loess slopes): ~550 mm/yr, June rain peak, deep chernozem on loess, Festuca rupicola–Stipa–Salvia nemorosa grassland, ground squirrels, mowing or grazing.
  2. **Pannonian sand steppe** (Kiskunság, Hungary): Calcaric Arenosol with >90 % sand and <1 % humus, Festuca vaginata open grassland, a groundwater table usually >2 m deep, and frequent wildfires.
  3. **Limestone rocky steppe hill** (Mikulov / Pálava type): thin Rendzic Leptosol over limestone, nests under stones, myrmecophile beetles in nests.
  4. **Vineyard edge / loess terrace** (Retz, Weinviertel): the unicolonial, non-swarming M. structor population is documented from Retz. Loess or chernozem soils, with tillage disturbance in the adjacent rows.
  5. **Central Anatolian steppe** (Ankara/Konya plateau, ~1,000 m): ~300–400 mm/yr mostly in winter and spring, calcareous brown soils (Kastanozem/Calcisol-like) on marl or gypsum, Thymus and cushion subshrubs, heavy sheep and goat grazing, Anatolian ground squirrels and blind mole rats.
  6. **Pontic feather-grass steppe** (Askania-Nova, Ukraine): 300–450 mm/yr, dark chestnut soil, Festuca–Stipa grassland. Fits M. muticus / M. ponticus.
- Slope and exposure: the hosts are "hot-dry" grasslands at the species' northern limit, so south- and southwest-facing slopes are the likely default in Central Europe. This is an inference; no source gave aspect data directly.

### Gaps
- AntWiki's Messor structor page (likely the best single habitat summary) was blocked by Cloudflare and could not be read.
- No source gave measured slope or aspect preferences, altitude limits for Central European M. structor s.str., or nest densities per hectare. The BOKU project measured nest density, but its results were not found.
- The Kiran & Karaman (2021) Central Anatolian ant fauna (Turk. J. Zool. 45:161–196; 383 localities, 24 habitat types) should list which Messor taxa occur in Anatolian steppe. The PDF was blocked, so Anatolian Messor species and habitats are not confirmed here.
- No frost-penetration depths were found for Hungary or Ankara.

## 2. Typical soil profiles in these habitats (horizons, texture, stones, roots, water) and what that means for burrowers

### Takeaway
The main soil "templates" are distinct and well documented:
- **Chernozem on loess:** a dark humus A horizon 20 cm to >1 m thick (55 cm in a Hungarian example), silty texture, lime at about 1 m, and many krotovinas (backfilled rodent burrows).
- **Kastanozem / calcareous steppe soil** (drier Anatolian and Pontic sites): a thinner brown A of about 25–50 cm, lime at about 1 m, gypsum at 1.5–2 m, and a permanently dry layer below the wetting depth.
- **Calcaric Arenosol** (sand steppe): very weak profile, >90 % sand, <1 % humus, groundwater usually >2 m deep.
- **Rendzic Leptosol** (limestone hills): <25–45 cm of dark soil directly on rock.

Texture and moisture control how deep and how fast burrowers dig. Blind mole rats dig deeper in moist soft soil, and ground squirrels track soil texture and stoniness.

### Cited Findings
**Chernozem (Pannonian and Pontic loess grassland)**
- Chernozems are "AhBC profiles with a dark brown to black mollic surface horizon over a cambic or argic subsurface horizon". The surface horizon "can be only 20 cm thick but extends down to a depth of more than 2 metres" in the best-developed profiles. Lime accumulates at about 1 m, gypsum may accumulate at 2–3 m (1.5–2.5 m in southern chernozems), and a "dead dry horizon" may occur at about 4 m — [FAO Lecture Notes on the Major Soils of the World](https://www.fao.org/4/y1899e/y1899e11.htm)
- The parent material is "uniformly textured, silty parent material (loess)". Soil fauna is very active, worm casts and krotovinas are common, and burrowing small vertebrates homogenise the soil. Krotovinas filled with dark humus stand out against the cinnamon-coloured subsoil — [FAO Lecture Notes](https://www.fao.org/4/y1899e/y1899e11.htm)
- Climate of chernozem steppe: continental, with annual precipitation roughly equal to evaporation (about 450–500 mm in the central steppe). The soils are well drained except in depressions with occasional shallow groundwater. Natural vegetation is tall-grass steppe — [FAO Lecture Notes](https://www.fao.org/4/y1899e/y1899e11.htm)
- A near-natural chernozem in Hajdúság (E Hungary) has a 55 cm chernic horizon with a gradual transition to slightly calcareous subsoil containing "numerous krotovinas". The upper 15 cm (Ah1) is practically carbonate-free, and the Ah2 below it is slightly compacted — [Hajdúság chernozem study, Geoderma Regional 2025 (abstract)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352009425000057)
- In Hungary, chernozems commonly form on loess or loess-like material under grassland. Their subtypes depend on mollic colour and the depth of carbonates; "pseudomyceliar" chernozems carry fine secondary carbonate at shallow depth — [Classification of the major soils of Hungary, Agrokémia és Talajtan 2006 (search snippet)](https://real.mtak.hu/98745/1/at_2006_55_1_19-28.pdf)

**Kastanozem (drier steppe: Anatolian plateau, southern Ukraine)**
- Kastanozems have a brown Ah of medium depth, about 50 cm in northern Eurasia and about 25 cm in the drier south, over a brown/cinnamon B. Lime accumulates at about 1 m and gypsum is common at 150–200 cm. Most have developed in loess. Krotovinas are present but less abundant than in chernozems — [FAO Lecture Notes](https://www.fao.org/4/y1899e/y1899e11.htm)
- The water regime is intermittent: soils dry to great depth in the dry season, with a "dead dry horizon" below the wetting limit, and run-off in heavy showers "can be considerable". Natural vegetation is short-grass steppe with many ephemerals and only 0.8–1 t/ha aboveground dry biomass — [FAO Lecture Notes](https://www.fao.org/4/y1899e/y1899e11.htm)
- Kastanozems occur where rainfall is 200–400 mm/yr, with calcium carbonate or gypsum accumulated at depth — [Britannica: Kastanozem](https://www.britannica.com/science/Kastanozem)
- Central Anatolian soils are mostly calcareous brown and reddish-brown soils — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Turkey/The-central-massif). Bedrock includes andesite, basalt, tuff, rhyolite, ignimbrite, flysch, marl, serpentine, limestone and gypsum — [PMC11532325](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11532325/)

**Phaeozem (moister forest-steppe margin)**
- The mollic horizon is 30–50 cm thick and secondary carbonates are absent from the upper metre. Earthworms and burrowing mammals homogenise the soil, and wormholes and krotovinas can extend into the C horizon — [FAO Lecture Notes](https://www.fao.org/4/y1899e/y1899e11.htm)

**Calcaric Arenosol (Pannonian sand steppe, Kiskunság)**
- Under Festuca vaginata sand grassland, the soil is a Calcaric Arenosol with a "very weakly developed" ACk–Ck profile. The 0–5 cm layer had 0.2 % organic matter and 11.3 % CaCO3 (pH ~8) — [Do Sandy Grasslands along the Danube… Preserve the Memory of Forest-Steppes?, Forests 12:114 (2021)](https://www.mdpi.com/1999-4907/12/2/114/htm)
- Pannonian sand-steppe soils are "poor in humus, belonging to the Arenosol type" — [EUNIS: Pannonian and Pontic sandy steppe (search snippet)](https://eunis.eea.europa.eu/habitats/8091). A review in the same search described them as Calcaric Arenosol with >90 % sand and <1 % humus. The exact source page was not verified, and the units (humus vs organic matter) differ from the 0.2 % figure above.
- In two southern Hungarian Arenosol profiles, groundwater stayed deeper than 2 m all period at Kelebia. At Kiskundorozsma, groundwater or its capillary fringe could reach the subsoil in humid years. The Kelebia profile stores only ~90 mm of water up to field capacity — [Assessing Available Water Content of Sandy Soils, Water Resour. Manage. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-020-02747-6)

**Rendzina / Rendzic Leptosol (limestone and dolomite steppe hills)**
- A Ukrainian classification divides rendzinas by profile thickness: poorly developed (<25 cm), short-profile (25–45 cm), full-profile (>45 cm). Another source gives a humus profile of about 30–70 cm on carbonate bedrock — [Ukrainian Carpathian rendzina study (search snippet)](https://publications.lnu.edu.ua/collections/index.php/carpathians/article/download/4874/5383)
- Serbian rendzinas are defined by an A–AC–C–R profile on parent rock with >20 % calcareous material — [Characteristics of rendzina soils in Serbia (search snippet)](https://aseestant.ceon.rs/index.php/jas/article/view/22369)

**Soil and burrowers**
- Blind mole rats (Spalax/Nannospalax) in Turkey build mostly one-layer tunnel systems, but three-layer systems in soft moist soil. Tunnels in moist soil are deeper than in hard soil. The animals mix soil with urine to make a sticky mud that strengthens gallery walls — [Yağcı, Coşkun & Aşan 2010, Zoology in the Middle East (abstract)](https://acikerisim.dicle.edu.tr/items/d0738d86-4730-491f-91bf-21a902bae203/full)
- Anatolian ground squirrel nest-burrow density in Isparta province varied with stoniness: highest at about 9–9.5 % stoniness, with 82 % occurrence on loamy-clay soils — [Isparta 2024 study (search snippet)](https://avesis.isparta.edu.tr/yayin/2d03d2f2-146d-40a9-b32b-261ae33f00ef/research-on-the-relationship-between-the-density-of-anatolian-ground-squirrel-spermophilus-xanthoprymnus-bennet-1835-nest-burrows-and-soil-texture)
- In eastern Austrian grasslands, humus content together with A-horizon depth explained 38 % of the variance in white-grub (Phyllopertha horticola) density — [Phyllopertha larvae in eastern Austrian grasslands, SpringerPlus 2015 / PMC4375213](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4375213/)
- Cicada nymphs need uncompacted substrate to build burrows and feeding cells, which is why they are used as indicators of soil health — [review via search snippet, Frontiers Ecol. Evol. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/ecology-and-evolution/articles/10.3389/fevo.2023.1219636/pdf)
- Snow strongly insulates soil. In South Moravia (Pannonian edge), even 1 cm of snow substantially reduces the temperature range at 5 cm depth and so limits frost penetration — [Rožnovský & Brzezina 2017, Contrib. Geophys. Geodesy](https://journal.geo.sav.sk/cgg/article/view/175)

### Inferences
- **Suggested 2D layer templates (0–200 cm), built from the cited depths:**
  - *Loess chernozem:* 0–15 cm crumbly black Ah1 (densest roots, worms, seeds); 15–55 cm black Ah2, slightly compacted; 55–100 cm transitional brown AC with dark-filled krotovinas; ~100 cm lime-pseudomycelia (Ck) loess, pale yellow-buff, soft and easy to dig, holds tunnel shape well. No groundwater in the top 2 m except in depressions.
  - *Sand steppe:* 0–5/10 cm grey AC with almost no humus; below that, uniform calcareous yellow sand (Ck). Tunnels collapse unless the soil is moist. Groundwater >2 m in dry years and nearer the subsoil in wet years, so a "rising damp" event fits a wet year. Low water storage (~90 mm) means rapid drought.
  - *Rendzina hill:* 0–20/30 cm black, stony, crumbly A over cracked limestone (R). Nests sit under flat stones and run along rock fissures, so stone slabs form a hard-rock layer at 25–45 cm.
  - *Anatolian calcareous steppe (Kastanozem/Calcisol):* 0–25 cm brown A; 25–100 cm cinnamon B; ~100 cm white lime layer (calcic, hard when dry); 150–200 cm gypsum. A permanently dry layer lies below the summer wetting depth, and run-off and crusting happen in summer storms.
- "Krotovinas" (old rodent burrows backfilled with dark topsoil) are a real, visually distinctive feature of chernozem and kastanozem profiles. They would make good pre-placed "soft soil veins" that ants or other diggers exploit.
- Moisture-dependent digging (deeper tunnels in moist soil, per the mole-rat data) supports making excavation speed and tunnel stability depend on both texture and moisture.

### Gaps
- No measured frost depth for Pannonian or Anatolian sites was found; snow insulation is documented only qualitatively. The designer would need national meteorological data.
- No source gave root-density-by-depth profiles for these grasslands, or stone content by horizon, beyond the qualitative "stony" rendzina descriptions.
- WRB classification of specific Central Anatolian steppe profiles (Calcisol vs Kastanozem vs Leptosol) was not confirmed.
- The specific Polish steppe rendzina profile (mollic horizon 0–20 cm under Stipa pennata / Festuca valesiaca) appeared in a search result, but its URL could not be confirmed, so it is not cited.

## 3. Surface vegetation, seed rain timing and size, and the soil seed bank

### Takeaway
- **Vegetation by site:** Pannonian loess steppe is dominated by Festuca rupicola / F. valesiaca with Stipa species (S. capillata, S. pulcherrima, S. joannis) and forbs such as Salvia nemorosa. Sand steppe is Festuca vaginata grassland. Anatolian steppe is a mosaic of grasses, Thymus and cushion subshrubs that shifts with grazing.
- **Seed rain** is concentrated in late spring to summer: grasses flower in May–June and set seed in June–July. Stipa capillata and late forbs extend the supply into autumn. Messor also cuts seeds straight off plants before they fall.
- **Seed sizes:** seeds harvested by Messor range from 0.08 mg to ~96 mg. Preferences cluster around 1–50 mg, and Stipa capillata seed weighs 8.4 mg.
- **Seed bank:** the 0–10 cm soil of Hungarian loess grassland holds ~20,000–23,000 viable seeds/m², mostly of a few weedy forbs and Poa, not the dominant bunchgrasses.

### Cited Findings
**Vegetation composition**
- Pannonian loess steppe belongs to the alliance Festucion valesiacae and is "rich on Stipa species (Stipa capillata, Stipa pulcherrima, Stipa joannis)" with Salvia nemorosa. It is sensitive to grazing and trampling — [EUNIS: Pannonic loess steppic grassland](https://eunis.eea.europa.eu/habitats/1002)
- The characteristic loess grassland association is Salvio nemorosae–Festucetum rupicolae. Its grasses are Festuca rupicola, Bromus inermis, Koeleria cristata, Stipa capillata, Alopecurus pratensis and Poa angustifolia. Loess-specialist forbs include Filipendula vulgaris, Fragaria viridis, Pimpinella saxifraga, Salvia nemorosa, Thymus glabrescens, Trifolium striatum and Veronica prostrata. The semi-natural grassland held 27.0 species/m² — [Tóth & Hüse 2014](https://www.aloki.hu/pdf/1202_537547.pdf)
- Degraded loess pastures (Cynodonti–Poëtum angustifoliae) are dominated by grazing-tolerant Cynodon dactylon, Poa angustifolia, Festuca pseudovina, F. rupicola and Carex stenophylla, plus Convolvulus arvensis, Galium verum and Euphorbia cyparissias. "At heavily grazed sites, thistles dominate (Eryngium campestre and Ononis spinosa)" — [Tóth & Hüse 2014](https://www.aloki.hu/pdf/1202_537547.pdf)
- Pannonian sand steppe: Festuca vaginata open grassland on Calcaric Arenosol — [Forests 12:114](https://www.mdpi.com/1999-4907/12/2/114/htm)
- Central Anatolian steppe vegetation types are grasslands dominated by grassy plants, dry steppes dominated by chamaephyte (sub)shrubs, forest-steppe with Quercus and Pyrus elaeagnifolia, and saline steppes. The model functional groups were trees, spiny and non-spiny subshrubs, perennial forbs, perennial grasses and annual herbs — [PMC11532325](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11532325/)
- Askania-Nova protects a Festuca–Stipa grassland. Its productivity depends more on the previous autumn–winter–spring precipitation than on the 12-month total — [Ekológia 2017 (search summary)](https://reference-global.com/article/10.1515/eko-2017-0020)

**Phenology and seed traits (verified factsheets)**
- Festuca valesiaca agg.: height 0.2–0.7 m, flowering May–June ("start of early summer" phase), diaspore = fruit/infructescence, mainly autochorous — [Pladias factsheet](https://www.pladias.cz/factsheet/default/6502)
- Stipa capillata: height ~0.63 m, flowering period May–October, seed mass 8.41 mg, epizoochorous (awned seeds hook onto animals) — [FloraVeg factsheet](https://floraveg.eu/en/factsheet/default/132964)

**What Messor harvests (relatives, Mediterranean semi-desert analogue)**
- In the Tabernas Desert (Spain; 243 mm rain, 17.3 °C), seed predation by ants was confirmed for 41 plant species. Seed masses ranged from 0.08 mg to 95.9 mg. The polyphagous Messor species took "all available seed types" from the soil surface and also cut them "directly from the mother plant" (pre-dispersal predation) — [Hensen 2002, Anales de Biología 24](https://revistas.um.es/analesbio/article/view/31421)
- Families and fruiting months in that list, with mean diaspore mass — [Hensen 2002, Table 2](https://revistas.um.es/analesbio/article/view/31421):
  - Poaceae: Stipa capensis 4.87 mg (V–VI), Stipa parviflora 1.93 mg (IV–VI), Stipa tenacissima 9.03 mg (IV–VI), Avena barbata 5.41 mg (V–VI), Bromus rubens 3.01 mg (V–VI).
  - Asteraceae: Centaurea aspera 2.22 mg, Centaurea melitensis 1.31 mg, Launaea 0.8–1.3 mg (all IV–VI).
  - Brassicaceae: Diplotaxis 0.11 mg (IV–VI).
  - Cistaceae: Helianthemum 0.1–0.68 mg (IV–VI).
  - Lamiaceae: Thymus 0.081 mg (IV–VI).
  - Plantaginaceae: Plantago 1–1.6 mg (V–VII).
  - Fabaceae: Anthyllis 2.69 mg (V–VIII), Hippocrepis pods 10.8 mg, Retama 95.9 mg (VI–X).
  - Dipsacaceae: Scabiosa 6.81 mg (V–VI).
  - Chenopodiaceae: Salsola 3.41 mg (VIII–XII).
- Messor barbarus prefers seeds of 4–50 mg regardless of seed density and uses only a subset of the plant community. It also selects by seed length — [Plowes et al. 2013](https://myrmecologicalnews.org/cms/index.php?option=com_download&view=download&filename=volume18%2Fmn18_33-49_printable.pdf&format=raw)
- M. structor forages individually on widely scattered seeds and recruits nestmates (via Dufour gland secretions and stridulation) to dense seed patches. It has been described as using broad (>1 m) trails with low forager density, where dropped seeds are later recovered. Foragers show strong site fidelity — [Plowes et al. 2013](https://myrmecologicalnews.org/cms/index.php?option=com_download&view=download&filename=volume18%2Fmn18_33-49_printable.pdf&format=raw)

**Soil seed bank (Hungarian loess grassland, 0–10 cm cores)**
- Mean seed bank density was 22,800 seeds/m² (degraded pasture) and 20,200 seeds/m² (semi-natural grassland). The cores were sampled as 0–5 and 5–10 cm layers. Seed-bank species per m² were 17.0 and 15.4 — [Tóth & Hüse 2014](https://www.aloki.hu/pdf/1202_537547.pdf)
- Dense seed banks belonged mostly to disturbance-tolerant forbs: Galium verum 4,268/m², Achillea collina 2,090/m², Conyza canadensis 6,764/m², Carex praecox/stenophylla 2,486/m². The dominant grasses had sparse seed banks (Festuca rupicola 685/m², Poa angustifolia 1,061/m², Cynodon 545/m²). Loess specialists had almost none (Salvia nemorosa 66/m², Filipendula vulgaris 0) — [Tóth & Hüse 2014](https://www.aloki.hu/pdf/1202_537547.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Suggested seed-rain calendar for a Pannonian loess or sand site.** This is an inference from the flowering months above, the Mediterranean fruiting months, and a ~3–6 week flowering-to-seed lag:
  - April–May: early ephemerals, Brassicaceae and Veronica; small seeds of 0.1–0.5 mg.
  - **June–July: main pulse.** Festuca, Poa, Koeleria, Bromus and early Stipa caryopses (2–9 mg), plus Thymus nutlets and Helianthemum.
  - July–September: Stipa capillata (8.4 mg, long awn), Salvia, Fabaceae pods, late Asteraceae achenes such as Centaurea and Achillea.
  - September–November: Chenopodiaceae/Salsola-type and Artemisia seeds on drier and saline Anatolian or Pontic steppe, plus leftover seed bank.
  - Winter: no fresh seed rain. The colony relies on stored granaries.
- Anatolian sites should have a **shorter, earlier pulse** (most rain falls in winter and spring, so the main seed set is likely May–June), then a long dry summer of seed-bank scavenging. Pannonian sites with a June rain peak could support a second autumn flush.
- Seeds in the soil: ~20,000 seeds/m² in the top 10 cm, about 70–80 % of them (by the table) small forb seeds (Galium, Conyza, Achillea). In 2D this suggests a sparse "buried seed" resource that ants rarely use, compared with abundant surface seed in season.
- Stipa awns are hygroscopic and twist (documented for related genera), which plausibly helps seeds self-bury. It is not confirmed for S. capillata in these sources.

### Gaps
- **No field study listing the plant species harvested by M. structor s.str. was found.** The Hahn & Maschwitz (1985, Oecologia) M. structor (= rufitarsis) foraging study and the Schlick-Steiner et al. (2005, Insectes Sociaux) Retz life-history study likely contain this, but neither was accessible.
- No month-by-month seed-rain trap data from Pannonian or Anatolian steppe were found, so the calendar above is inferred.
- Anatolian steppe dominant species (e.g., Festuca valesiaca, Stipa holosericea, Bromus tomentellus, Astragalus cushions) could not be confirmed from an accessible source. The synoptic Anatolian vegetation paper returned 403.

## 4. Soil and surface fauna a nest would meet: densities, depths, and interactions with ants

### Takeaway
A square metre of Pannonian grassland soil holds tens of thousands of mites and springtails in the top 10 cm (Mesostigmata alone ~8,600/m²), around 100+ earthworms in moister grassland, up to a few hundred scarab grubs/m² in outbreak patches, and the burrows of voles, ground squirrels and (in Anatolia and the Pontic steppe) blind mole rats. Messor nests also host specialised guests: silverfish, ant crickets, woodlice, mites and beetles, including the Central European Messor-nest beetle Attaephilus molitori. The ants are also hunted by specialist ant-eating spiders (Zodarion), which mainly target Messor in Iberia.

### Cited Findings
**Microarthropods**
- In Marchfeld grassy fallows (Pannonian, chernozem soils), predatory mites (Mesostigmata) in the top 10 cm numbered 3,050–13,460 m⁻² (mean 8,560 m⁻²), with Gamasina making up 97.7 %. The paper notes that mites and Collembola together reach "hundreds of thousands of individuals per m²" in soil (citing Koehler 1999) — [Marchfeld predatory mites study, PMC3365242](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3365242/)
- Springtails typically number about 10,000–100,000 per m² — [Encyclopédie de l'environnement: Collembola](https://www.encyclopedie-environnement.org/en/life/collembola-actors-of-soil-life/). A German post-mining site study reports about 28,000–30,000 Collembola/m² — [Soil fauna as indicators in post-mining sites (search snippet)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228912405_Soil_fauna_Lumbricidae_Collembola_Diplopoda_and_Chilopoda_as_indicators_of_soil_ecosubsystem_development_in_post-mining_sites_of_Eastern_Germany)

**Earthworms**
- Permanent grasslands in Slovakia averaged about 120 earthworms/m², nearly twice arable land (~62/m²), and more than double the biomass — [Sustainability 8:906 (2016)](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/8/9/906)
- In chernozems, "soil fauna is very active; worm casts and krotovinas are common" — [FAO Lecture Notes](https://www.fao.org/4/y1899e/y1899e11.htm)

**Insect larvae**
- White grubs (Phyllopertha horticola) in eastern Austrian grasslands reached a maximum of 303 grubs/m² — [PMC4375213](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4375213/). A forest-nursery guide treats >1 grub per 0.1 m² (~10/m²) as damaging — [USDA forest nursery pests: white grubs](https://npn.rngr.net/publications/forest-nursery-pests/conifer-and-hardwood-insects/white-grubs/at_download/file)
- Mole crickets (Gryllotalpa stepposa / G. unispina, steppe species): burrows at the surface in the warm season; winter burrows reach 50–100 cm. In G. unispina, overwintering adults sit in a chamber at the bottom of a 120 cm vertical gallery and larvae overwinter at 70–80 cm. Females build nests at 10–20 cm — [AgroAtlas: Gryllotalpa stepposa](https://agroatlas.ru/en/content/pests/Gryllotalpa_stepposa/index.html); [AgroAtlas: G. unispina](https://agroatlas.ru/en/content/pests/Gryllotalpa_unispina/index.html)
- Cicada nymphs feed on root xylem for several years and need uncompacted soil — [Frontiers Ecol. Evol. 2023 (search snippet)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/ecology-and-evolution/articles/10.3389/fevo.2023.1219636/pdf)

**Burrowing mammals**
- **Blind mole rats (Spalax / Nannospalax; Anatolia, Ukraine, Pannonia):** feeding tunnels at 10–25 cm, with deeper chambers at 120–320 cm. There are one or two grass-lined nest chambers, usually 4–9 storerooms, and empty chambers — [Spalax microphthalmus species account, Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/6608891). Upper feeding passages lie at 10–15 cm, joined to the nest level by 2–4 vertical corridors — [AgroAtlas: Spalax microphthalmus](https://agroatlas.ru/en/content/pests/Spalax_microphthalmus/index.html). Turkish populations mostly have one-layer systems, three layers in soft moist soil, and linear or scattered mound types — [Yağcı et al. 2010](https://acikerisim.dicle.edu.tr/items/d0738d86-4730-491f-91bf-21a902bae203/full)
- **Anatolian (Asia Minor) ground squirrel, Spermophilus xanthoprymnus:** lives in steppe and alpine meadow at 800–2,900 m. Regular burrows are simple tunnels about 21–57 cm deep; nesting or hibernation burrows reach 219 cm, with extra entrances and deep nest and food-storage chambers. It eats mainly seeds, leaves and shoots (including wild grains), sometimes small invertebrates, and hibernates generally September–March. Predators: eagle owls, buzzards, falcons, red foxes — [Wikipedia: Asia Minor ground squirrel (citing Gür & Gür 2010, Mammalian Species)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor_ground_squirrel). It spends about half the year hibernating — [Gür & Gür 2015, Mammalian Biology 80:265–272 (record)](https://openaccess.ahievran.edu.tr/xmlui/handle/20.500.12513/2104)
- **European ground squirrel, Spermophilus citellus (Pannonian grasslands):** a Czech relict population held 4.0–17.5 individuals/ha and 5.6–32.2 burrow entrances per individual — [Matějů et al. 2008, Lynx](https://publikace.nm.cz/en/periodicals/lns/39-2/ecology-and-space-use-in-a-relict-population-of-the-european-ground-squirrel-spermophilus-citellus-at-the-north-western-edge-of-its-distribution-range-ekologie-a-prostorove-usporadani-v-reliktni-populaci-sysla-obecneho-spermophilus-citellus)- **Common vole, Microtus arvalis:** 50 excavated Swiss burrow systems averaged 16.9 m of tunnel (range 0.5–70.2 m), with nests and food caches at the deepest point, mostly central — [Brügger et al. 2010, Mammalia (search snippet)](https://boris.unibe.ch/5756/1/mamm.2010.035.pdf). During outbreaks, densities "can simultaneously rise up to several thousand individuals per hectare" — [Development of a forecast model for outbreaks of common voles (JKI)](https://www.openagrar.de/servlets/MCRFileNodeServlet/Document_derivate_00008434/Development%20of%20a%20forecast%20model%20for%20outbreaks%20of%20common%20voles.pdf). Czech data put outbreaks above 6,000/ha, with cyclic explosions every 2–5 years — [Plant Protection Science 2025](https://pps.agriculturejournals.cz/pdfs/pps/2025/04/07.pdf). (A German afforestation-site figure of 5,550 holes/ha and ~2,300 m of burrow per ha at 60 voles/ha also appeared in the search results, but its exact source was not verified.)

**Nest guests (myrmecophiles) and predators**
- **Attaephilus molitori** (Leiodidae, Cholevinae) was found in M. cf. structor nests under stones on steppe at Mikulov (Czech Republic) in late May. It is a myrmecophile described from near Vienna, also known from NW Bulgaria, and "restricted to xerophilous habitats with occurrence of Messor cf. structor" — [Růžička & Vávra 2012](https://coleoptera.fzp.czu.cz/pdf/Ruzicka&Vavra2012Klapalekiana_Leiodidae_Attaephilus_Moravia.pdf)
- In Maltese Messor nests (mainly M. capitatus) the guests recorded were (counts across about 10 nests) — [Maltese intranidal myrmecophiles, PMC9864695](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9864695/):
  - ant crickets Myrmecophilus ochraceus (~38 specimens, tolerated, staying at the nest periphery);
  - the Messor-specialist silverfish Neoasterolepisma crassipes, which mimics host hydrocarbons and lives in the upper chambers under rocks;
  - Cholovocera beetles living in "seed waste dumps", likely eating seeds or fungi;
  - myrmecophile woodlice: Porcellionides myrmecophilus (~30) and Platyarthrus schoblii (~81);
  - a Messor-specialist predatory mite, Gymnolaelaps messor.
- In the same study, 14 of 30 obligate myrmecophiles (~47 %) occurred in Messor nests. Messor was described as an "ecosystem engineer" whose seed stores support granivores, detritivores, mycetophages and predators — [PMC9864695](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9864695/)
- **Zodarion ant-eating spiders:** gut-content metabarcoding of three Iberian Zodarion species found diets "largely based on the granivorous Messor ants" — [Arachnology.cz record of the 2022 study](https://www.arachnology.cz/literatura/gut-content-metabarcoding-of-three-widespread-iberian-ant-eating-spiders-reveals-specialisation-on-the-same-abundant-harvester-ants.-3953.html). Zodarion cyrenaicum (Negev) feeds almost exclusively on Messor arenarius, paralyses prey larger than itself with a single bite, and mimics the ant — [Naturwissenschaften 2014 study](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00114-014-1189-8); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/06/140612095035.htm)

**Neighbouring ants (competition/interaction)**
- Messor capitatus marks colony-specific "anal spots" around the nest, which may separate the foraging areas of neighbouring colonies. In M. aciculatus, encounters between neighbours lead to ritualised duels without recruiting nestmates — [Plowes et al. 2013](https://myrmecologicalnews.org/cms/index.php?option=com_download&view=download&filename=volume18%2Fmn18_33-49_printable.pdf&format=raw)

### Inferences
- **Suggested interaction table** (inferred roles; the densities are from the cited sources):
  - Springtails and mites, 10⁴–10⁵/m² in the 0–10 cm "living crust": mostly neutral background. Some mites are nest predators or commensals (Gymnolaelaps), and springtails and fungal mites likely live on mouldy granary seed.
  - Earthworms (~50–120/m², more in moister loess, few in dry sand): neutral. They loosen soil and make wet-season channels.
  - Scarab grubs (0–300/m², patchy): neutral root-feeders. When dead they become insect carcass food.
  - Mole crickets, mole rats and voles: tunnel "disturbance agents" that cut ant galleries.
  - Ground squirrels: seed competitors that dig burrows to 0.5–2.2 m.
  - Zodarion spiders: predators at nest entrances.
  - Myrmecophile beetles, woodlice and silverfish: living inside nests as scavengers or kleptoparasites of stored seed.
- Krotovinas in chernozem show that rodent burrowing reshapes the profile over centuries. A game event in which a ground squirrel or mole rat tunnel slices through the ant nest is realistic.
- For an Anatolian site, the ground squirrel's 6-month hibernation (Sep–Mar) and its seed diet make it a seasonal surface seed competitor, absent in winter.

### Gaps
- No density data specific to steppe chernozem or Kastanozem soil fauna were found; the figures come from Austrian, Slovak and German grasslands. Earthworms are likely much scarcer in dry sand and Anatolian soils, but no number was found.
- No data were found on wireworm (Agriotes) densities, cicada nymph depths and densities in European vineyards, or antlion predation on Messor.
- No direct observation of Central European M. structor myrmecophile communities beyond Attaephilus; the Maltese list is M. capitatus-dominated.
- European mole (Talpa europaea) tunnel depths and densities were not researched (moles avoid very dry sand and steppe; unverified).

## 5. Other food sources for a mostly granivorous ant: insect carcasses, honeydew, fruit, flower parts

### Takeaway
Seeds dominate the Messor diet. In the one quantified field study (two Spanish Messor species), dead arthropods made up only ~2–3 % of items carried in, and the ants also brought in fruits, capsule walls, petals, glumes and awns, many of them probably not food. Messor workers also harvest seeds and whole fruits directly from plants, ripe or not. No source documents honeydew-tending by Messor.

### Cited Findings
- Of 3,100 returning Messor foragers in Tabernas, only ~50 % carried food items. Item composition — [Hensen 2002, Table 4](https://revistas.um.es/analesbio/article/view/31421):
  - Messor bouvieri: dead arthropods 1.82 %; Helianthemum almeriense seeds 47.4 % plus fruits 7.6 %; Launaea lanifera 24.7 %; Thymus fruits 10.6 %; Stipa tenacissima 3.9 %.
  - M. timidus: dead arthropods 2.59 %.
- Foragers "frequently brought in" different plant particles, "presumably not serving as food, such as leaves, petals, and parts of infrutescences like capsule walls, glumes, or awns". Whole fruits of several species were "cut off regardless of whether they are already ripe or not" — [Hensen 2002](https://revistas.um.es/analesbio/article/view/31421)
- Parts that are not food are carried to a waste pile next to the nest — [Hensen 2002](https://revistas.um.es/analesbio/article/view/31421)
- Colony middens of harvester ants "create micro-environments that influence the abundance, distribution, and diversity of plants" — [Plowes et al. 2013](https://myrmecologicalnews.org/cms/index.php?option=com_download&view=download&filename=volume18%2Fmn18_33-49_printable.pdf&format=raw)
- The Plowes et al. review contrasts liquid harvesters (honeydew, hemolymph, nectar; e.g., Myrmecocystus honey ants) with seed harvesters such as Messor. It does not credit Messor with honeydew collection — [Plowes et al. 2013](https://myrmecologicalnews.org/cms/index.php?option=com_download&view=download&filename=volume18%2Fmn18_33-49_printable.pdf&format=raw)
- Ant-keeping sources say M. structor eats mainly seeds and takes insects as an occasional protein supplement, with no figures given (non-peer-reviewed) — [Ant-shack care sheet](https://www.ant-shack.com/blogs/ant-care-sheets-1/messor-structor-the-steppe-harvester-ant)

### Inferences
- Reasonable game parameters, inferred from the Spanish data: about 95–98 % of food intake as seeds and fruits, about 2–3 % as insect carcasses (scavenged, not hunted), and occasional flower parts and chaff carried in and dumped on the midden.
- Fallen soft fruit (e.g., grapes at a vineyard edge) is not documented as Messor food. If used, it should be flagged as speculative. Grape seeds dropped by birds or at harvest would be a more plausible "vineyard bonus" seed resource (also unverified).
- The midden (chaff ring) around the entrance is a visible, realistic surface feature. It also creates a fertile microhabitat where plants germinate.

### Gaps
- No quantified diet data exist for M. structor s.str. (or M. ponticus / M. muticus) in the sources found.
- No evidence was found either way on honeydew or aphid tending, nectar use, or fallen-fruit use by Messor structor.
- What fraction of carcasses are scavenged versus killed was not found.

## 6. Surface events and disturbances: grazing, trampling, fire, drought, storms, snow and frost, ploughing

### Takeaway
Real disturbance regimes differ sharply between sites:
- **Grazing:** sheep, goats and cattle in Anatolia reduce forbs and grasses and favour Thymus and spiny subshrubs. Overgrazing removes subshrubs and boosts annuals.
- **Heavily grazed Pannonian pasture** turns thistle-dominated (Eryngium, Ononis).
- **Wildfire** is increasingly frequent in Kiskunság sand steppe. The grass layer recovers within a few years, but shrubs do not.
- **Ploughing / tillage** reduces Messor nest numbers, can kill colonies, and buries surface seeds.
- **Climate stress:** hot summer soil (>40 °C at the surface) shuts down foraging by day, while drought and snow cover shape the soil's thermal and water state.

### Cited Findings
**Grazing and trampling**
- In Central Anatolia, domestic grazing is mostly by cattle, sheep and goats. Grazing "significantly reduces the cover of forbs and grasses", with forbs most affected, while Thymus increases in grazed areas — [PMC11532325](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11532325/)
- In the same paper's model, overgrazing at about 4× carrying capacity causes complete loss of spiny and non-spiny subshrubs and perennial forbs, while annual herbs increase. Moderate grazing favours spiny over non-spiny subshrubs — [PMC11532325](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11532325/)
- Pannonian loess steppe is "sensitive to grazing and trampling" and much of it has been converted to other grassland types — [EUNIS](https://eunis.eea.europa.eu/habitats/1002). Heavily grazed Hungarian loess pastures become thistle-dominated (Eryngium campestre, Ononis spinosa) — [Tóth & Hüse 2014](https://www.aloki.hu/pdf/1202_537547.pdf)

**Fire**
- Wildfires "have become more frequent in natural habitats of the Kiskunság Sand Ridge". After a fire that burnt half of a sand grassland–juniper–poplar forest-steppe, the grass layer recovered "quickly but incompletely" within 2.5 years, the juniper canopy did not regenerate, and spider and carabid communities shifted to post-fire species — [Samu et al. 2010, Community Ecology 11(2)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1556/ComEc.11.2010.2.1); [FRAMES catalogue](https://www.frames.gov/catalog/52693)
- In a burned grassland, the seed-eating ant Tetramorium caespitum became dominant while Lasius alienus nest density fell. The authors link these shifts to food availability; the location was not confirmed as Pannonian — [Sondej & Domisch 2024](https://jukuri.luke.fi/bitstream/10024/554523/1/Sondej_Domisch_2024.pdf)

**Ploughing / tillage (Messor barbarus in Spanish dryland cereals, closest analogue)**
- Nests were regularly spaced in no-till fields but randomly distributed in tilled fields (two passes a year), consistent with tillage disturbing or killing colonies — [Harvester ant nest distribution depends on soil disturbance regime, Univ. Lleida](https://recercat.cat/handle/10459.1/64721)
- Tillage lowered nest numbers compared with no-till. Summer tillage lowered seed predation and buried most surface weed seeds — [Baraibar PhD thesis summary, UdL](https://www.grem.udl.cat/node/177)
- Ant and nest densities rose with years under no-till, peaked at 11–12 years, then declined — [UdL 2011 study (search snippet)](https://repositori.udl.cat/handle/10459.1/58623)

**Heat, drought and daily activity**
- Messor bouvieri and M. timidus start collecting in the evening once the soil surface drops below 40 °C. They forage at a steady level through the night, peak again just after sunrise, and stop after a few hours as the soil heats up. Soil temperatures during foraging ranged 12.0–42.1 °C (air 11–29 °C) — [Hensen 2002](https://revistas.um.es/analesbio/article/view/31421)
- The share of active colonies fell from 91–100 % in May to 9–13 % by mid-June, even though temperatures did not rise further — [Hensen 2002, Table 3](https://revistas.um.es/analesbio/article/view/31421)
- M. barbarus foraging is concentrated in the morning. As temperatures rise, foragers cut trail use — [Foraging strategy quick response to temperature of Messor barbarus (search snippet)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/23265146_Foraging_Strategy_Quick_Response_to_Temperature_of_Messor_barbarus_Hymenoptera_Formicidae_in_Mediterranean_Environments)
- In a drought year, M. barbarus trail networks form longer links and fewer branches, which enlarges the search area. After a productive season they form more branching and shorter links — [Plowes et al. 2013](https://myrmecologicalnews.org/cms/index.php?option=com_download&view=download&filename=volume18%2Fmn18_33-49_printable.pdf&format=raw)
- In Kastanozem steppe, run-off during heavy showers "can be considerable", and soils dry to great depth in the dry season — [FAO Lecture Notes](https://www.fao.org/4/y1899e/y1899e11.htm)
- Askania-Nova grassland productivity is driven by autumn–winter–spring precipitation — [Ekológia 2017](https://reference-global.com/article/10.1515/eko-2017-0020)

**Snow and frost**
- Even 1 cm of snow substantially damps soil temperature swings (South Moravia), so snow cover acts as an insulator against frost penetration — [Rožnovský & Brzezina 2017](https://journal.geo.sav.sk/cgg/article/view/175)
- Ankara's January mean is −2 °C. Anatolian ground squirrels hibernate September–March, about half the year — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Turkey/The-central-massif); [Wikipedia / Gür & Gür](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor_ground_squirrel)
- Steppe mole crickets overwinter at 50–120 cm, below the frost zone — [AgroAtlas](https://agroatlas.ru/en/content/pests/Gryllotalpa_unispina/index.html)

### Inferences
- **Suggested event deck per site**, inferred from the findings:
  - *Anatolian steppe:* passing sheep and goat flocks (trampling collapses near-surface tunnels; grazing removes seed heads before they fall); winter snow and frost; spring rain pulses; summer drought with daytime surface >40 °C, which turns foraging crepuscular or nocturnal; occasional violent convective storms with run-off and crusting.
  - *Pannonian loess grassland:* mowing (a sudden drop in standing seed heads plus a pulse of dropped seed); cattle or sheep grazing; a June storm peak; snow-insulated winters.
  - *Sand steppe:* wildfire (removes litter and seeds on the surface, while the grass layer regrows within 1–3 years); fast-draining drought; wind.
  - *Vineyard edge:* tillage of adjacent rows 1–2× per year (destroys the shallow parts of nests, buries surface seeds); possibly herbicide (not researched).
- Foraging hours should follow the soil-surface temperature window, roughly 12–40 °C, so the simulation would show night and dawn foraging in midsummer.
- Deep chambers below about 50–100 cm are buffered from frost (by analogy with mole-cricket overwintering depths) and from heat. The ants can retreat deep for winter, while shallow tunnels are exposed to trampling and ploughing.

### Gaps
- No direct data on how grazing or trampling affects Messor nests or forager mortality.
- No data on fire mortality of Messor colonies, or on how deep nests survive fire or ploughing.
- No frost-depth or snow-cover-day statistics for Pannonian or Anatolian sites.
- No storm, flooding or nest-inundation data for Messor.
- Disturbance regimes for Ukrainian steppe (fire frequency, grazing by wild ungulates at Askania-Nova) were not researched.
