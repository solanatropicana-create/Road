# Threats, Competitors, Parasites, Diseases and Nest Guests of Harvester-Ant Colonies (Messor structor first; other Messor, Pogonomyrmex and seed-harvesters for comparison)

> Scope note for the report writer: every bullet under "Cited Findings" has an inline source. Anything without a source sits under "Inferences" (my reasoning, including design suggestions for the soil cross-section) or "Gaps". **Region/genus flags:** [PAL] = Palaearctic Messor evidence; [MED] = Mediterranean Messor other than M. structor; [NA] = North American Pogonomyrmex/Veromessor/Novomessor; [GEN] = general ant biology from other genera.
>
> **Taxonomy caveat that affects every "Messor structor" record:** Steiner et al. (2018, *Mol. Phylogenet. Evol.* 127:387–404) split European "M. structor" into five species: M. structor s.str., M. ibericus, M. muticus, plus new M. ponticus and M. mcarthuri ([Steiner et al. 2018 PDF](https://www.antvid.org/PDF/Steiner_et_al._-_2018_-_Molecular_Phylogenetics_and_Evolution.pdf)). A Russian regional checklist doesn't accept the split and keeps M. structor in the broad sense ([rusant checklist](https://rusant-lv.myspecies.info/taxonomy/term/54)). An earlier study had already found two lineages in Central Europe ([Schlick-Steiner/Steiner et al. 2006, EJE](https://www.eje.cz/pdfs/eje/2006/02/25.pdf)). So older "M. structor" records may belong to any of these species. For a game this changes little biologically.

## 1. Competitors: other ants near Messor structor, battles between neighbouring Messor colonies, seed robbing, brood raiding, killing of founding queens

### Takeaway
Messor's main rivals are other Messor colonies, and other ant species come second. Neighbouring colonies overlap and fight. The fights can be ritualised boxing or mass battles that leave decapitated workers. Neighbours rob each other's seed loads and granaries, and a stronger colony has been seen destroying a weaker one and carrying off its seeds. Other species, and Messor itself, block rival nest entrances with soil and pebbles. Established colonies' foragers kill founding queens they find. Brood raiding between incipient colonies is documented in the lab for (Vero)Messor pergandei, but disputed in the field.

### Cited Findings
**Intraspecific (colony vs colony) [MED/PAL/NA]**
- *Messor aciculatus* (East Asia, a Palaearctic species): colonies had **no territories** and neighbours' foraging areas overlapped. Hostile meetings were uncommon. When they happened, workers did **ritualised combat in which the winner pushed out the loser without injury**. **Seed robbery** was common: outsiders attacked residents carrying seeds and took the seeds, and sometimes **entered nests to steal stored seed**. Robbing frequencies pointed to a **dominance order among neighbours**, and in one case **a stronger neighbour destroyed a weaker colony and moved its stored seeds into its own nest** — [Springer, Insectes Sociaux, 1990s M. aciculatus study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2FBF01245701) (I believe the author is Yamaguchi 1995, but the snippet didn't confirm it).
- *M. aciculatus* workers box opponents with their forelegs in an intraspecific ritual fight — [Mercier, Dejean & Lenoir, Actes Coll. Insectes Sociaux vol.10](https://dictionnaire-amoureux-des-fourmis.fr/Noms%20propres/Publis/1996/Actes-Colloques-Insectes-Sociaux-VOL10-Mercier-Dejean-Lenoir.pdf).
- *M. wasmanni* and *M. minor* (Italy): colonies showed **strong territoriality**, and overlap between them changed with the season — [Univ. Parma record](https://air.unipr.it/handle/11381/2295489).
- *Messor barbarus* (SE Spain, 16 June 2018): **two neighbouring colonies fought in open grass between their nests**. The arena held "a great number of workers from the two colonies, including **many decapitated, dead and injured ants**." The "scent of war" drew parasitoid phorid flies, which attacked for ~40 min until sunset (see §4) — [Delgado, Disney & Palma 2020, Bull. Insectology 73:193–200](https://archive.bulletinofinsectology.org/pdfarticles/vol73-2020-193-200delgado.pdf).
- *Messor andrei* (California) [NA]: a paper on **nest relocation and encounters between colonies**. The abstract wasn't accessible — [Brown 1999, Insectes Sociaux 46:66–70](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/en/publications/nest-relocation-and-encounters-between-colonies-of-the-seed-harve).
- *Pogonomyrmex barbatus* [NA]: foraging trails of neighbouring colonies meet **about once a day**. **Fighting is rare and happens mostly on very humid days after summer rains.** "Fights can go on for 30 minutes or more, as one ant latches onto the petiole of another and attempts, usually unsuccessfully, to bite it in two", which puts both fighters at risk of desiccation — [Sundaram, Steiner & Gordon 2022, Ecol. Monogr.](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10316491) (full text: [PDF](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10316491)).
- *P. barbatus* behaviour toward neighbours **changes with colony age**. Foragers of 1–2-year-old colonies **retreat**. Foragers of 3–4-year-old colonies, which are growing fast, **engage in repeated confrontations**. Mature colonies (≥5 yr) **avoid overlapping trails**. When a colony was enclosed, its neighbours soon foraged in its former area — [Sundaram et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10316491).
- *P. occidentalis* [NA]: colonies **lose foragers in encounters with neighbouring colonies** (De Vita 1979). Survival rises with distance to the nearest neighbour, especially for small colonies, and **direct interference may "self-thin" the population into an evenly spaced pattern** (Wiernasz & Cole 1995) — [Wikipedia summary of primary literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pogonomyrmex_occidentalis).
- In *P. barbatus*, crowding by conspecific neighbours (less foraging area per colony) lowers a colony's chance of surviving to the next year, and this crowding effect **grew stronger during drought** — [Sundaram et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10316491).

**Killing of founding queens by established colonies**
- *P. occidentalis*: **foragers kill queens they meet above ground and occasionally dig queens out** (Billick et al. 2001) — [Wikipedia, P. occidentalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pogonomyrmex_occidentalis).
- General: after a nuptial flight "the vast majority of virgin queens die within hours… most are destroyed by **predators and hostile workers of alien nests**", and the rest drown, overheat or dry out — [AntWiki, Nuptial Flights and Mating (Hölldobler & Wilson, The Ants)](https://antwiki.org/wiki/Nuptial_Flights_and_Mating).

**Brood raiding and queen fights during founding**
- *Veromessor (=Messor) pergandei* [NA]: in Rissing & Pollock (1987), incipient colonies founded by several queens **brood-raid and defeat colonies founded by one queen**. In the lab, single foundresses given extra workers and pupae raid more successfully. Queens are strictly single-founding (haplometrotic) at a California site but group-founding in Arizona — [Ryti 1988 summary, Psyche](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/mac/projects/psyche/92/92-125.html); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF01240970). **Contested:** Pfennig's field study found **no brood raiding in the field**, and Bernasconi & Strassmann note field-confirmed brood raiding only in *Solenopsis invicta* — [Univ. Neuchâtel reply](https://libra.unine.ch/bitstreams/b0247c26-66ef-410a-b19f-2777c3a77de5/download). Group founding in V. pergandei is linked to **lower rainfall**, and recruitment of new colonies is rare — [Johnson 2020/21, Ecol. Evol.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7790620).
- *Messor semirufus* [MED, Israel]: queen groups mostly form when **a queen invades another's founding chamber to displace the resident**. Queens in such groups die at a **considerably higher rate** than solitary queens, which the authors put down to the high cost of finding a site and digging — [Hebrew Univ. record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/forced-associations-by-young-queens-of-the-harvester-ant-messor-s/).

**Interspecific interference (other ant species)**
- *Messor capitatus* [MED, Italy] has a task group of **"sappers"**. They gather at the entrances of **other species'** nests and use threat displays, fighting and **plugging the holes with earth, pebbles or other materials**. The target colony "may be completely blocked as long as the sappers patrol the nest". M. capitatus also disrupts others' foraging tracks, **steals food at nest entrances**, and takes over rich food sources until rivals barricade themselves inside — [Grasso et al. 2004, Ethol. Ecol. Evol. 16:197–207](https://air.unipr.it/handle/11381/1441792).
- *Messor barbarus* **steals harvested *Euphorbia characias* seeds from *Tapinoma nigerrimum*** by taking them directly from carriers, and also through territorial threats, chemical deterrence and nest-plugging — [Wikipedia, Messor barbarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messor_barbarus) (secondary source).
- *Novomessor (Aphaenogaster) cockerelli* [NA] **plugs P. barbatus nest entrances at night, especially those of small young colonies**. The plugged colony stays dark inside and emerges late. Midday heat then cuts its foraging short, which leaves more seed for the plugger that night. The two species also take over each other's abandoned nests — [Sundaram et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10316491); [Barton, Sanders & Gordon 2002, Am. Midl. Nat.](https://bioone.org/journals/the-american-midland-naturalist/volume-148/issue-2/0003-0031(2002)148[0376:TEOPAC]2.0.CO;2/The-Effects-of-Proximity-and-Colony-Age-on-Interspecific-Interference/10.1674/0003-0031(2002)148[0376:TEOPAC]2.0.CO;2.full).
- *Dorymyrmex bicolor* [NA] workers (as few as 5, often 10–30) **drop stones into nest entrances of Myrmecocystus** honey ants (Möglich & Alpert 1979). This is a model for "stone-dropping" interference, though it was **not shown against Pogonomyrmex** — [Wikipedia, Dorymyrmex bicolor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorymyrmex_bicolor).
- *Cataglyphis bicolor* [MED, Algeria] mostly scavenges dead insects, often ants. **Messor barbarus made up 87% and 82.2% of prey items** in two nests, but only ~0.37% of biomass, so most of what it takes are corpses or small items — [Biodiversity Journal 2019, 10(2):101–108](https://www.biodiversityjournal.com/pdf/10(2)_101-108.pdf).
- *P. occidentalis* [NA]: the honey ant *Myrmecocystus mexicanus* feeds on **dead or nearly dead workers**, with no good evidence that it attacks healthy ones (Cole et al. 2001) — [Wikipedia, P. occidentalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pogonomyrmex_occidentalis).
- Thief ants: *Solenopsis fugax* (Europe) nests next to larger ants and **steals their brood through its own narrow tunnels**. During raids it **releases a long-lasting repellent from its poison gland** that stops the hosts defending their brood — [BWARS species account](https://bwars.com/node/34); [AntWiki, Thief ants](https://antwiki.org/wiki/Thief_ants).
- North American review: the old view that competition for seeds between species *structures* harvester-ant communities is now thought to be overstated — [Annual Review of Entomology 2001 on seed-harvester ants (ASU-hosted)](https://www.asu.edu/clas/sirgtools/annual%20review%20entomology-2001.pdf) (from the search snippet; the PDF link was dead when I tried it). In P. barbatus, the sharp drops of the sympatric seed/arthropod foragers *Messor (Veromessor)* and *N. cockerelli* suggest those species had **little effect on P. barbatus survival** — [Sundaram et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10316491).

### Inferences
- **Most important competitor = conspecific neighbour.** Across Messor and Pogonomyrmex the evidence agrees: conflict with neighbours ranges from avoidance through ritual pushing and seed robbing to rare battles. Crowding (less foraging area) is the long-term killer, not single battles. In a game, a slow "foraging area squeeze" can sit alongside rare dramatic "border war" events.
- **Cross-section depiction (inference):** Battle = a surface strip between two mounds filling with grappling pairs (a worker clamped onto a rival's petiole for 30+ min); corpses later carried to the midden. Seed robbing = rival workers intercepting loaded foragers on the trail. Granary raid = enemy workers entering the shallow tunnels and leaving with seeds. Colony destruction = the victor's workers stream down into the loser's granaries. Nest plugging = small pebbles and soil appear in the entrance shaft at dawn, the colony's own workers dig them out from below, and the day's foraging starts late.
- **Trigger conditions (inference):** Pogonomyrmex fights cluster on humid days after summer rain. A game can raise battle chance after rain, when many workers are on the surface.
- **Stage-dependence (inference from P. barbatus):** very young colonies retreat, fast-growing colonies quarrel, mature colonies avoid. That makes a clean three-stage AI rule.

### Gaps
- No primary study of territoriality, battles or seed robbing in **M. structor s.str.** itself. The Austrian unicolonial population (no aggression between nests; see §8) suggests behaviour may vary by population.
- No found data on Messor vs *Tetramorium* (pavement ants), *Lasius niger*, *Formica* (e.g., F. cunicularia), *Camponotus*, or *Cataglyphis* killing live Messor, or on raids on Messor brood. Pavement-ant mass battles are well known in general, but I found no Messor-specific source.
- No record of *Solenopsis fugax* or other thief ants living inside Messor nests specifically.
- No measured rate of seed robbing or colony takeovers per year.

## 2. Seed competitors: rodents, birds, ground beetles; robbing of ant granaries

### Takeaway
Rodents, granivorous birds and carabid beetles compete with harvester ants for the same seed rain. The best experimental evidence comes from North American deserts, where removing rodents or ants raised the other group's density in the short term. Mediterranean studies show rodents, birds and ants split seeds by size and microhabitat. I found **no documented case of rodents or birds digging into Messor granaries**. The documented granary thieves are **other ant colonies**.

### Cited Findings
- [NA] Brown & Davidson's desert work: rodents and ants overlap heavily in seed use, and **rodents removed much more seed than ants**. Diet overlap, population increases when either group was excluded, and complementary diversity patterns all pointed to competition — [weecology portal: Brown 1977 / 1979](https://portal.weecology.org/publication/brown1979experimental/); [Utah collections](https://collections.lib.utah.edu/ark:/87278/s61n8jnj).
- [NA] Sonoran Desert removal experiments (Davidson et al. 1984): removing either group raised the other's density in the short term. But **ant populations on rodent-removal plots declined relative to controls starting about two years in**. The proposed reason is indirect facilitation: rodents eat the large-seeded plants, which otherwise crowd out the small-seeded plants ants depend on — [Davidson et al. 1984 PDF](https://www.sfu.ca/biology/courses/bisc830/Davidson_etal_1984.pdf).
- [MED] Southern Spain: rodents and granivorous ants are both important seed predators. **Ants avoided seeds heavier than ~20 mg; rodents ignored seed size.** Rodents foraged mostly **under trees and shrubs** and avoided open ground, while ants did the reverse — [Springer, Oecologia (Hulme)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s004420050212) (which sentence belongs to which paper in the search set is not fully certain).
- [MED] In central Spain, *Messor barbarus* dominates seed harvesting in grassland and shares seed with *M. bouvieri* in scrubland, where their nests and foraging areas are partly segregated — [Azcárate & Peco 2003, Insectes Sociaux](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00040-003-0635-y).
- [MED] Cuenca mountains (Spain): **birds were the main seed predators in years of heavy seed production; rodents were least important.** Seed removal was far higher in years of low seed production — [Dialnet PDF](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/3366344.pdf).
- [GEN, temperate Europe] Ground beetles (Carabidae) are **probably the most important invertebrate seed-eaters in the temperate zone** — [Eur. J. Entomol. 2003](https://eje.cz/pdfs/eje/2003/04/13.pdf).
- [Southern Africa] In Karoo rangeland, harvester ants took ~4.1 million seeds (5.2 kg)/ha/yr, about **10% of seeds and 18% of the annual seed biomass** of common plants — [Rangelands Gateway](https://rangelandsgateway.org/dlio/8659).
- Granary robbing **by other ants** is documented (M. aciculatus neighbours entering nests and taking stored seed; one stronger colony emptying a weaker one's granaries) — [Springer, M. aciculatus](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2FBF01245701).

### Inferences
- Seed competitors are best modelled as **indirect pressure**: they lower the seed density on the surface map, so foragers come home empty more often and granary income falls. In drought years this adds to the food shortage that drives colony death in Pogonomyrmex (§8).
- Partitioning rules for a game: ants win in **open ground** and on **small seeds (<~20 mg)**, rodents win **under shrubs** and on large seeds, birds win in **bumper seed years**, and carabids (e.g., *Harpalus*, *Amara*) take seeds on the surface at night. The beetle genera come from the brief; they aren't confirmed by a sourced finding here.
- A "rodent digs into the granary" event would be speculative. If used, it should be rare and flagged as fiction.

### Gaps
- No quantitative study of rodent, bird or carabid competition with **M. structor** in Eurasian steppe (e.g., susliks *Spermophilus*, hamsters *Cricetus*, larks).
- No primary record found of vertebrates digging out Messor granaries.
- No *Harpalus*/*Amara* vs Messor study found.

## 3. Predators of workers, foragers, alates and founding queens

### Takeaway
Messor foragers are hunted by specialist ant-eating spiders (*Zodarion*, which in Iberia and the Negev feed mainly or almost only on Messor), by antlion larvae, and in North America by horned lizards. Alates and newly mated queens suffer the heaviest predation (birds, spiders, robber flies, dragonflies, hostile ants). Predation on workers is a chronic drain that mature colonies absorb. Some species pause foraging after heavy losses, and others ignore them.

### Cited Findings
**Specialist spiders [MED/PAL]**
- Gut-content metabarcoding of **three widespread Iberian *Zodarion* species** found diets **mostly of Messor**, with other genera secondary. All three are strictly ant-eating, and their wide ranges may follow abundant Messor — [Pekár lab 2022, arachnology.cz record](https://www.arachnology.cz/literatura/gut-content-metabarcoding-of-three-widespread-iberian-ant-eating-spiders-reveals-specialisation-on-the-same-abundant-harvester-ants.-3953.html?jazyk=en).
- *Zodarion cyrenaicum* (Negev, Israel) feeds **almost exclusively on *Messor arenarius***, and juveniles already choose this large ant. Adults and juveniles paralyse prey with **a single bite**. Adult venom glands are **>50× larger** than juveniles', and paralysis takes **~1.5× longer** with a juvenile bite — [Pekár et al. 2014, Naturwissenschaften](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00114-014-1189-8); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/06/140612095035.htm).
- A **crab spider capturing a recently dealated *Pogonomyrmex maricopa* queen** is shown in Hölldobler (1976). The mortality of colony-founding queens is "extremely high" — [AntWiki, Nuptial Flights and Mating](https://antwiki.org/wiki/Nuptial_Flights_and_Mating) [NA].

**Antlions [GEN]**
- *Myrmeleon bore* (Japan): ≥30 prey species, mostly ants, and **ants were 81.1% of first-instar captures**. A Florida *Myrmeleon* study found ants ~25% of diet, and one ant genus stole prey from pits in ~10% of cases — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF02361201); [Florida Entomologist](https://journals.flvc.org/flaent/article/view/58214).

**Lizards [NA, with a Palaearctic gap]**
- Horned lizards (*Phrynosoma*) ate mainly *P. desertorum* and *P. rugosus*, taking **a few ants at each colony** and mostly ants away from nest discs or columns. In artificial-predation experiments, **P. desertorum stopped foraging for up to five days after losing 25% of its foragers, while P. rugosus did not respond**. Lizard numbers appear limited by colony productivity, and colonies effectively **replace their whole worker force each year** — [Whitford & Bryant 1979, Ecology](https://archive.jornada.nmsu.edu/files/bibliography/79-Ecology-Whitford.pdf).
- Munger (1984): heavier harvesting did not lower long-term colony yield, and lizard predation may account for the yearly turnover of foragers. Raising artificial predation **reduced alate production**, while fencing out lizards had no effect — [Springer, Oecologia](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00317186); [Jornada bibliography](https://archive.jornada.nmsu.edu/files/bibliography/81-The%20American%20Midland%20Naturalist-Whitford.pdf).
- Desert horned lizards "trap-line" between colonies, mostly in cool morning and evening hours — [WWU student project](https://www.biol.wwu.edu/anderson/projects/PosterFinalEditPp&Ants2003.htm).
- Ant-eating in lizards is concentrated in certain iguanian families, which don't include the Palaearctic lacertids *Psammodromus*/*Acanthodactylus* — [EBSCO abstract, 2024 lizard myrmecophagy study](https://www.ebsco.com/articles/zoology/9f2c1051-4028-5ec3-bb8c-a6abad3c34db/myrmecophagy-in-lizards-evolutionary-and-ecological-implications).

**Predation on alates / mating swarms**
- Most alates die within hours of leaving the nest: "destroyed by predators and hostile workers of alien nests", or drowned, overheated or desiccated — [AntWiki, Nuptial Flights and Mating](https://antwiki.org/wiki/Nuptial_Flights_and_Mating).
- [NA] At *Formica subpolita* male swarms, ~900 predation events were recorded. The most common predator was the **robber fly *Efferia staminea***, and prey was overwhelmingly male. The authors compare this with Pogonomyrmex — [Psyche 101:93](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/mac/projects/psyche/101/101-093.html).
- [MED] *Messor barbarus* (Iberia) mating flights spread over ~7 weeks in **September–October**, in **eight clusters set off by the same weather fronts** — [Univ. Girona record](https://dugi-doc.udg.edu/handle/10256/11940).
- Founding queens that must forage (semi-claustral) face predation risks that fully claustral queens avoid. *Messor andrei* queens lack the storage proteins used by claustral queens, so they probably forage — [Oecologia 2002 (ASU)](https://www.asu.edu/clas/sirgtools/Oecologia%20-2002.pdf) [NA].
- Founding harvester queens face **predation and desiccation**, with mortality that "may reach 99%" (citing Gordon & Kulig 1996 among others) — [PMC2803755](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2803755).

### Inferences
- **Ranking of predators by effect on a Messor colony (inference):** (1) predators of alates and founding queens; they decide whether new colonies exist at all. (2) *Zodarion* spiders: Messor-specialist, nocturnal-to-crepuscular foraging-trail hunters, with a steady low worker toll. (3) Antlions on sandy foraging ground. (4) Lizards, birds and toads, which are opportunists in the Palaearctic (unquantified).
- **Cross-section depiction (inference):** a *Zodarion* waits near the trail or entrance, bites once, the forager stiffens, and the spider drags it to a retreat. An antlion funnel appears in sandy soil beside the trail and a worker slides in. A lizard visits a few times a day and takes several foragers each time. Depending on the colony's "temperament" (the P. desertorum vs P. rugosus contrast), foraging either pauses for 1–5 days or carries on.
- Since colonies replace their worker force annually, worker predation should cost *production* (fewer seeds, fewer alates) more than it threatens colony survival, unless the colony is still small.

### Gaps
- No quantitative Palaearctic data on birds (bee-eaters, larks, wryneck, green woodpecker), toads (*Bufotes viridis*), lacertid lizards, robber flies or carabid beetles preying on Messor workers or alates.
- No data on *Euroleon nostras* or *Myrmeleon formicarius* taking Messor.
- **M. structor nuptial-flight timing is poorly documented.** Hobbyist sites list March–September ([antkeeping.info](https://antkeeping.info/ants/messor-structor)), and an Austrian population is reported to lack swarming flights (see §8).

## 4. Parasitoids and parasites: phorid flies, eucharitid wasps, nematodes, mites, Strepsiptera, social parasites

### Takeaway
Messor has at least two confirmed parasitoid groups. One is the **phorid fly *Razorfemora zaragozae*** on *M. barbarus* in Spain, with good behavioural data, including a colony-wide "C-posture" defence. The other is **eucharitid wasps (*Eucharis*)**, whose larvae ride into the nest on workers and eat the pupae. Messor nests also hold specialist parasitic or predatory mites (*Gymnolaelaps messor*). I found **no mermithid nematode, Strepsiptera or workerless social parasite records for Messor**.

### Cited Findings
**Phorid flies [MED]** — all from [Delgado, Disney & Palma 2020, Bull. Insectology 73(2):193–200](https://archive.bulletinofinsectology.org/pdfarticles/vol73-2020-193-200delgado.pdf):
- *Razorfemora zaragozae* (Phoridae, body 2.2–2.3 mm) is a **parasitoid of *Messor barbarus*** in southern Spain (Murcia). Phorids are described as "the most important parasitoids" of ants (citing Johnson 2001).
- **Target choice:** females attack **medium minors (5–10 mm)** and avoid small minors (3–5 mm) and large-headed majors (10–15 mm). They also avoid stationary ants, ants in an aggressive display, and ants in the "C" posture. Laden and unladen ants are attacked alike.
- **Attack method:** the fly hovers along trunk trails and around entrances, lines up with the ant, and **jabs the gaster with its ovipositor**. It never hits the same ant twice. One fly made between a few and **30 hits** per bout. Up to **10 females** worked one colony along a ~7 m trail, usually 1–4.
- **Weather limit:** flies stop and retreat when wind exceeds **0.5–0.6 m/s**, and stop at dusk.
- **Season:** the first fly appeared on **4 May**, numbers peaked in **late June–early July**, and the flies were gone by **mid-July**.
- **Ant defence = "C posture"** (gaster curled under the body). A few workers took it from mid-March. By 4 May, **~90% of trail workers** were in C posture, and it scaled with the number of flies. **All three castes** adopt it, although only medium minors are attacked. M. barbarus **keeps foraging** in C posture, unlike Solenopsis, whose foraging is disrupted. In the lab, **a major worker caught and crushed a fly** in its mandibles.
- Flies were **drawn to a battle between two M. barbarus colonies**, attacking active isolated workers but not fighters or injured ants (see §1).
- Parasitism rates are unknown: no fly pupae were found, ~120 discarded head capsules had no puparia, and numbers parasitised per nest are usually low in other systems.
- **M. barbarus annual cycle at the site:** activity resumes mid-February, trunk trails form in late Feb–March and are established by early May, the colony aestivates mid-July to August (active mainly in the evening and at night), surface activity resumes mid-September, and the season ends late October–November.

**Eucharitid wasps [MED/PAL]**
- *Eucharis adscendens* and *E. punctata* are recorded as parasitoids of Messor, including *M. barbarus* — [AntWiki Messor page via search snippet](https://antwiki.org/wiki/Messor) (genus-level records, exact host species uncertain).
- Eucharitid life cycle: females lay eggs **in plant tissue**. The active first-instar larva (planidium) attaches to a **worker ant and is carried into the nest**. It waits until the host larva reaches the **prepupa or pupa** before feeding. Each species usually attacks just one ant genus — [Wikipedia, Eucharitidae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucharitidae); the prepupa/pupa feeding detail comes from a University of Calicut-hosted review — [scholar.uoc.ac.in](https://scholar.uoc.ac.in/server/api/core/bitstreams/f3f60f3b-713c-423e-b96d-d678c8962c66/content).

**Mites [MED]**
- *Gymnolaelaps messor* (Laelapidae) is known only from Messor nests (Malta, *M. capitatus*). Its ecology is little known, and laelapids are generally thought to be predators. *Pogonolaelaps canestrinii* is also recorded with Messor — [Cassar, Lapeva-Gjonova & Mifsud 2023, Insects 14(1):45](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9864695/).

**Nematodes [GEN only]**
- Mermithid nematodes turn ants into "mermithergates" (altered morphology). Records include *Myrmica*, *Lasius*, where *Pheromermis myrmecophila* **reduces wing size of parasitised sexuals**, and *Pheidole*. A tetradonematid infects **up to 5%** of some *Cephalotes atratus* populations — [Wikipedia, Mermithergate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mermithergate); [Univ. Limerick record](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/observations-on-mermithid-parasitism-nematoda-mermithidae-in-two-/). No Messor or Pogonomyrmex record surfaced.

**Social parasites**
- Inquilines in general are workerless, wholly dependent on their hosts, and produce only sexuals. Their populations are small, cryptic and possibly short-lived — [Messer, Cover & Rabeling 2020, PMC7109158](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7109158/). My searches found **no described social parasite of Messor**.

### Inferences
- **Game event "Phorid season" (Mediterranean-style climate):** from about May to mid-July, small flies hover over the trunk trail on calm days. Medium workers curl into the C posture, foraging continues with a small efficiency penalty (inference; not measured), and a few workers carry a larva that later kills them. Windy days and dusk switch the event off. A parasitised worker leaving the nest to die fits the social-immunity "leave to die" pattern (§5), but this was **not observed** for Messor.
- **"Eucharis hitchhiker":** planidia picked up by foragers on flowerheads or seeds get carried down to the brood chambers and later consume pupae. This is a hidden brood loss that can be shown as a pupa that never ecloses.
- Social parasitism and mermithids are best kept as **fictional/rare** options for Messor, or framed as "known from other genera".

### Gaps
- No parasitism rates for *Razorfemora* or *Eucharis* in Messor colonies, and no phorid record for *M. structor*.
- No search results on Strepsiptera (Myrmecolacidae) in Palaearctic Messor.
- No Messor-specific mermithid records.
- No confirmed list of Pogonomyrmex inquilines from a source in this session. From memory, *P. anergismus* and *P. colei* are workerless inquilines in other Pogonomyrmex, but this is unverified here.

## 5. Pathogens: entomopathogenic fungi, Laboulbeniales, social immunity defences, granary moulds and germinating seed

### Takeaway
M. structor carries at least two fungal parasites, both recorded in the Balkans. One is the ectoparasitic Laboulbeniales ***Rickia lenoirii*** (sublethal; a related *Rickia* in Myrmica raises stress mortality). The other is the endoparasitic ***Myrmicinosporidium durum***, whose dark spores fill the body cavity. Soil fungi such as *Metarhizium* and *Beauveria* kill ants within days, and *M. barbarus* dies at high rates when exposed in the lab. Ants defend through social immunity: grooming, removing corpses, and dying workers leaving the nest. Messor also keeps its granaries dry and **disables seeds (removing appendages, biting off radicles)** so they don't sprout. Possible microbial symbionts may suppress mould.

### Cited Findings
**Fungi recorded on Messor [PAL/MED]**
- ***Rickia lenoirii*** (Laboulbeniales, ectoparasite) found on **M. structor, M. wasmanni, M. hellenius and M. mcarthuri** in Albania, Bulgaria and continental Greece. ***Myrmicinosporidium durum*** (Blastocladiomycota, endoparasite) was recorded for the **first time in M. structor (Bulgaria)** — [Lapeva-Gjonova, Csősz & Báthori 2022, Diversity 14:1132](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/675aa0090ce2cede71cbe6be). Prevalence wasn't available in the abstract.
- *M. durum*: almost global, with a broad and growing host list. Infection is recognised by **dark, thick-walled spores filling the haemocoel, visible through the cuticle**. Its life cycle and transmission are poorly understood. It is "more common in Europe than previously thought" (Csősz et al. 2012) — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myrmicinosporidium_durum); [Csősz et al. 2012, PMC3637036](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3637036/). Hosts include *M. barbarus* (Portugal) and *M. capitatus* (Spain, new record) — [Ecoticias news](https://www.ecoticias.com/naturaleza/hongo-parasito-de-hormigas-castilla-leon-hallazgos-mundo).
- Analogue: ***Rickia wasmannii* on Myrmica** [GEN], long thought harmless. Infected workers are **more sensitive to food and water deprivation**, drink more, and are **less aggressive and less bold**. Infected workers die more in the lab, and Dutch field prevalence averaged ~38% — [real.mtak.hu](https://real.mtak.hu/27531/); [real.mtak.hu](https://real.mtak.hu/135260); [Sci. Rep. 2021](https://doaj.org/article/71bc28a8e12a4d34ad1d128613ac78ca). (Several sources were returned together, so which figure comes from which paper isn't fully certain.)
- ***Metarhizium brunneum* on *Messor barbarus***: a Nottingham thesis found **high mortality of ants exposed to M. brunneum** while studying the metapleural gland's role, with an unclear, possibly passive, protective mechanism — [Univ. Nottingham eprint 80708](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/80708) (search snippet; the page wouldn't load).
- [GEN] *Metarhizium* and *Beauveria* infect fire ants in the field and lab, and **infected insects typically die within a few days** — [USDA-ARS](https://www.ars.usda.gov/Research/docs.htm?docid=9007). In *Formica selysi*, *Beauveria bassiana* occurred in **17% of colonies**. Surprisingly, young queens **preferred nest sites contaminated** with *B. bassiana* and *M. brunneum* — [RERO doc](https://doc.rero.ch/record/314968?ln=fr). *Atta* queens collected after mating flights carried *Beauveria*, which sporulated on dead queens, so **newly mated queens pick up fungi** at the start of founding — [UNESP repository](https://repositorio.unesp.br/items/0601d544-6aba-44f2-b8be-fb43c2394d9e/full).

**Social immunity defences [GEN]**
- Framework: colony-level defences cut disease entry and spread, and are both **prophylactic and activated on demand** — [Cremer, Armitage & Schmid-Hempel 2007, Curr. Biol. "Social immunity"](https://research-explorer.ista.ac.at/record/3909); updated in Cremer, Pull & Fürst 2018, Annu. Rev. Entomol. 63:105–123 (same group, [ISTA](https://ista.ac.at/en/research/cremer-group/)).
- **Allogrooming** removes spores and ectoparasites when they are detected on a nestmate — [PMC4126623](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4126623).
- **Corpse removal (necrophoresis):** in *Myrmica rubra*, colonies allowed to remove corpses had **higher adult survival from day 8 onward**. When restricted, workers moved corpses **away from brood into nest corners** — [Diez et al. 2014, Biol. Lett.](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/lifesci/insectnavigation/documents/biol-lett-2014-diez-.pdf). Intermittent foragers do most corpse handling and then **groom themselves more**, acting as "hygienists" — [2024 study, PMC11632371](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11632371/). A death-associated chemical (**oleic acid**) triggers corpse carrying — [Passera 2019 review](https://insectes-sociaux.univ-tours.fr/dictionnaire-amoureux-des-fourmis/Noms%20propres/Publis/2019/Passera-2019-Revue_155SHNT_Fourmis_infection.pdf).
- **Dying workers leave:** in *Temnothorax unifasciatus*, fungus-infected, CO2-shortened and spontaneously dying ants **all isolated themselves from the nest hours to days before death** — [Heinze & Walter 2010, Curr. Biol. 20:249–252](https://pred.uni-regensburg.de/id/eprint/25158/). Press reports put it at **1,600+ workers from 28 colonies, 92% of spontaneously dying workers left the nest** — [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/terminally-ill-ants-choose-to-die-alone) (secondary). Nest gatekeepers **didn't reject** diseased nestmates, which isolated themselves instead — [Leclerc & Detrain 2016, Sci. Nat.](https://dipot.ulb.ac.be/dspace/bitstream/2013/234774/3/20LeclercDetrain2016SciNat.pdf).

**Granary hygiene and germination [PAL/MED]**
- Messor stores seeds in granaries **kept dry, which prevents germination** — [Wikipedia, Messor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messor).
- *M. structor* workers **remove seed appendages before storage, which inhibits germination** (Pacini 1990) — [Wikipedia, M. structor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messor_structor).
- Early observers recorded harvester workers **biting off the radicle** to stop germination before storing seeds — [Wikipedia, Messor barbarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messor_barbarus) (secondary).
- After rain, Messor **carry seeds outside to dry, then re-sort them**. This is hobbyist observation only — [Formiculture forum](https://www.formiculture.com/topic/19645-my-messor-barbarus-colony/page-9).
- *Messor orientalis*: seed stores stay relatively free of mould **despite damp conditions** (observed in an animal room). Two bacteria isolated from workers (*Brachybacterium phenoliresistens*, *Microbacterium* sp.) **inhibited fungi in vitro**, with 100% inhibition of *Peronophythora litchii* and *Rhizoctonia solani* at ≥30% broth. The role in granary protection is only a hypothesis — [Wu et al. 2022, Insects 13:691](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9408877/).
- Metapleural-gland antimicrobial function in harvester ants is **unresolved** — [search summary incl. Nottingham thesis](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/80708).

### Inferences
- **Disease events for the game (inference):**
  - *"Spore outbreak" (Metarhizium/Beauveria)*: more likely after warm wet spells in the soil. Infected workers turn sluggish, nestmates groom them (a short spike in grooming), sick workers walk out and die on the surface, and corpses go to the midden. Lethal to individuals within days. Colony-lethal only if hygiene is overwhelmed, for example when worker numbers are low in an incipient colony.
  - *"Dark-spore disease" (Myrmicinosporidium)*: rare, slow. Individual workers look darker and swollen. Mostly a chronic drain.
  - *"Fungal hair" (Rickia lenoirii)*: very common-looking, mostly harmless. It lowers stress tolerance during drought or starvation, which links it to drought events.
  - *"Wet granary"*: after rain or a flood, seeds in shallow granaries get damp. Workers haul them to the surface to dry. Sprouting seeds get their radicle bitten off, and mouldy seeds go to the refuse heap. If labour is short, granary stock is lost.
- Founding queens likely carry spores from the flight and soil (from Atta/Formica evidence), so a queen-death-by-fungus outcome is plausible in the founding chamber.

### Gaps
- No prevalence data for *R. lenoirii* or *M. durum* in M. structor colonies, and no measured colony-level effect.
- No field study of entomopathogenic fungi in M. structor nests.
- No peer-reviewed confirmation of post-rain seed drying in Messor; it rests on hobbyist reports only (as far as this search found).
- No data on how often granaries spoil, or how much stock is lost to germination or mould.

## 6. Myrmecophiles and nest guests in Messor nests

### Takeaway
Messor nests are hotspots for nest guests. In Malta, **14 of 30 obligate myrmecophile species (~47%) were hosted by Messor**, and 4 of the 5 single-genus specialists live only with Messor. The guests are mostly **scavengers and seed or debris feeders in refuse and granary chambers**: silverfish, Cholovocera beetles, Thorictus beetles, blind isopods. Ant crickets steal food. Laelapid mites are probably predators. Most are tolerated or ignored, some get through by chemical mimicry or appeasement, and at least one darkling beetle forces its way in.

### Cited Findings
Main source for Malta material: [Cassar, Lapeva-Gjonova & Mifsud 2023, Insects 14(1):45, "The Intranidal Myrmecophiles of the Maltese Islands with Notes on Messor Nests as Repositories of Biodiversity"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9864695/):
- **30 obligate myrmecophile species** recorded from nests of 13 ant species. **14 (~47%) are hosted by Messor.** **4 of the 5 species tied to a single ant genus live only with Messor**: *Gymnolaelaps messor*, *Neoasterolepisma crassipes* and two *Cholovocera*. Messor had **11 locally recorded associations plus 3 probable**, the most of any ant genus.
- **Why Messor:** nests are large, structurally complex and **stocked with seeds**, so seed-feeders, detritivores and fungivores on decaying plant matter, and predators can all coexist. Messor ranks among the genera with the highest share of unique symbionts in Europe, and the authors call it an ecosystem engineer.
- **Ant crickets:** *Myrmecophilus ochraceus* adults were only with *M. capitatus*, usually under stones over the nest rather than in deep chambers, often several per nest. The authors classify them as synoeketes (tolerated scavengers) that ants **treat with indifference**. *M. baronii* is historically with *Camponotus*, **occasionally with "Messor(?) structor"**.
- **Silverfish (Zygentoma):** *Neoasterolepisma crassipes* is a "**Messor specialist**", found in upper chambers under rocks, several per nest. It **keeps the host's cuticular hydrocarbon profile after moulting**, so it probably makes the chemicals itself (chemical mimicry). Also recorded: *N. wasmanni* and *Proatelurina pseudolepisma*.
- **Beetles:** *Cholovocera formicaria* (Endomychidae) is common in *M. capitatus* nests and prefers **seed waste dumps**, probably eating seeds or fungi. *C. fleischeri* is also present. *Thorictus grandicollis* (Dermestidae) **roams freely through nests eating plant debris and shows appeasement behaviour toward hosts**.
- **Isopods:** *Platyarthrus schoblii* (**32 specimens in one Gozo sample**), *P. caudatus*, *P. aiasensis* (recorded hosts include *M. capitatus* and "*Messor*(?) *structor*"), and *Porcellionides myrmecophilus*, which probably eats decaying matter, possibly stored seeds.
- **Mites:** *Gymnolaelaps messor* and *Pogonolaelaps canestrinii* (Laelapidae), probably predators.
- *Oochrotus unicolor* (Tenebrionidae, Mediterranean) is adapted to the **waste-dump niche of Messor nests**. In lab tests it **readily ate seeds and flour, poorly accepted other foods, and never ate ant brood** — [researchportal.be record](https://researchportal.be/nl/node/5784325) (search snippet; the page itself was blocked). In France it is known from **6 Mediterranean départements, 24 communes in total** — [Cabon & Soldati, Bull. Soc. ent. France 129(1)](https://lasef.org/wp-content/uploads/BSEF/129-1/2324_Cabon_&_Soldati.pdf).
- *Scaurus uncinus* (Tenebrionidae) enters ***M. barbarus* nests in SE Spain by brute force alone**, a "relatively primitive" strategy — [Eur. J. Entomol. 2024 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/04cafd95773d42488a40ce14c7421fca).
- [GEN] Ant-cricket (*Myrmecophilus*) behaviour: they are **kleptoparasites** that eat the ants' food and **trick workers into regurgitating** by imitating grooming and trophallaxis contacts. They also scrape oily secretions from ants. Hosts often react aggressively within ~4 mm and **kill and eat crickets they catch**. Crickets match host cuticular hydrocarbons — [Masloski & Greenwood 2013 (AntWiki PDF)](https://antwiki.org/wiki/images/2/20/Masloski_Greenwood_2013.pdf); [MEM Myrmecophilus paper](https://mem.org.msstate.edu/Researchtaxapages/Orthoptera/Orthop.papers/Myrmecophillus2009.pdf).
- Bulgaria: **33 rove-beetle species** (Staphylinidae) from nests of 11 ant species, mostly synoeketes or facultative myrmecophiles (Lapeva-Gjonova & Ilieff 2012, Acta Entomol. Slovenica). Whether Messor was among the hosts isn't clear from the snippet — [search summary](https://www.dlib.si/stream/URN:NBN:SI:DOC-K2ILFUKX/fbf8d945-b28a-4905-a4d1-a7100f495c32/TEXT).

### Inferences
- **Guest guild map for a cross-section (inference, built from the findings above):**
  - **Refuse/midden chambers and seed-husk dumps:** *Oochrotus*, *Cholovocera*, isopods (*Platyarthrus*, *Porcellionides*), *Thorictus*. Harmless recyclers that eat husks, mouldy seed and debris. They could even slightly *reduce* mould load (speculative).
  - **Upper chambers under stones:** silverfish (*Neoasterolepisma*) and ant crickets (*Myrmecophilus*). Food thieves that intercept trophallaxis; a small food drain. Crickets that get caught are eaten.
  - **Brood and worker chambers:** laelapid mites, probably predators of small arthropods or mites, maybe of eggs (unconfirmed).
  - **Surface "burglar":** *Scaurus* darkling beetles push into entrance tunnels and are attacked.
- **Ant response states:** *ignore* (synoeketes, mimics), *attack when detected* (crickets, intruding beetles), *appeased* (Thorictus). A simple "detection probability × aggression" rule would cover all three.
- Guest numbers should scale with **colony age and size and granary/refuse volume**; big old nests are "biodiversity reservoirs".

### Gaps
- No Messor-specific records of **Histeridae** or **Paussinae** surfaced. Paussines are mostly linked to *Pheidole* in the literature I know, but I didn't verify that here.
- No list of nest guests for **M. structor s.str.** in steppe or Central European habitats. The Malta data come mostly from *M. capitatus*.
- No data on colony-level cost (food stolen per guest).
- The *Atelura formicaria* association with Messor was not confirmed in this search.

## 7. Abiotic threats: flooding, nest collapse, drought, heat, frost, trampling, fire, ploughing; colony responses

### Takeaway
For long-lived harvester-ant colonies, the best-quantified abiotic killer is **drought (low rainfall → less seed)**, which cuts both recruitment and survival. **Tillage** can disturb or kill Messor colonies in fields. **Fire** is usually survived. Flooding responses are documented in other ants: earthen levees at entrances, moving brood, evacuation, rafting. For Messor specifically, flooding evidence is only anecdotal. Heat is handled by shifting activity times (aestivation, nocturnal foraging) and by moving brood up and down the deep nest.

### Cited Findings
**Drought and rainfall [NA, strongest data]**
- *P. barbatus*, ~300 colonies censused 1988–2019 (Rodeo, New Mexico): **recruitment (a founding colony surviving to age 1) depends on rainfall**. As rainfall fell from ~2001–2003 ("the most severe drought in the SW USA in 1000 years"), **recruitment and adult survival both declined**. Annual survival had a significant **dip in 2016, driven by "young adult" colonies (5–10 yr)**. Colonies increasingly died as young adults instead of living into their 20s — [Sundaram, Steiner & Gordon 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10316491).
- Colony mortality was higher in years of **low summer rainfall** (Sanders & Gordon 2004). **Alate production fell in crowded neighbourhoods after a dry year** — [Sundaram et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10316491).
- Colonies that **cut foraging on dry days to save water** were more likely to produce offspring colonies during drought (Gordon 2013). Water loss across the cuticle is ~98% of total water loss — [Sundaram et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10316491).

**Heat, cold, daily and seasonal activity**
- *M. barbarus* (SE Spain) **aestivates mid-July to August**, working mainly in the evening and at night, then resumes daytime activity mid-September and stops late October–November — [Delgado et al. 2020](https://archive.bulletinofinsectology.org/pdfarticles/vol73-2020-193-200delgado.pdf).
- *P. occidentalis* [NA] is active when mound surface temperature is **25–53 °C**. In summer it forages only **5–11 am and 3–9 pm**. Workers **move brood within the nest (up to 5 m deep) to reach safe temperatures**, and colonies stop producing brood before overwintering — [Wikipedia, P. occidentalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pogonomyrmex_occidentalis).
- *P. barbatus*: the colony's foraging is "inhibited by the midday heat" — [Sundaram et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10316491).
- Nest depth: *M. aciculatus* nests up to ~4 m deep — [AntWiki, M. aciculatus](https://antwiki.org/wiki/Messor_aciculatus). *M. arenarius*: one excavation reached ~2 m, with first granaries and brood near 2 m — [The Wild Martin (hobby/ecology site)](https://thewildmartin.com/ant-ecology/messor-arenarius-care-guide-and-ecology). No measured depth for M. structor was found.
- Captive M. structor overwintering is given as ~15 °C, from end of November to end of February (hobbyist care sheets) — [ameisenhaltung.de](https://www.ameisenhaltung.de/artenverzeichnis/entry-pdf-export/237-messor-structor/); [antcube](https://antcube.shop/cs/?p=2725).

**Flooding and rain [GEN + anecdotes]**
- Floodplain ants (Argentina, 5 species from different genera) **build earthen levees around nest entrances after heavy rain**. In 1 cm of standing water, intact levees kept water out in 4/4, 2/2, 7/8 and 17/17 nests of four species, and failed in *Pheidole jelseki*. **Breached levees doubled recruitment time** (8.0→16.8 min in *Dorymyrmex*) and **raised prey-retrieval time from 2.3 to 10.7 min** (*Ectatomma*). Eight hours after flooding, breached colonies had a median of **0 foragers vs 5**, and **6 workers on nest repair vs 0** — [LeBrun, Moffett & Holway 2011, Insectes Sociaux 58:263–269](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3059759). The paper's introduction also lists **rafting, nest evacuation, bailing and physiological tolerance** as other responses.
- *Monomorium antarcticum* (New Zealand): under simulated rain, workers **moved brood from cavities into tunnels and returned it after the rain**, prioritising cavities with more brood — [Otago record](https://ourarchive.otago.ac.nz/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Subterranean-nesting-behaviour-in-response-to/9926495644801891).
- Messor anecdote: a flooded test tube killed all workers of a young captive *Messor* colony while the queen survived — [Formiculture forum](https://www.formiculture.com/topic/17098-messor-barbarus-workers-dying/) (anecdotal).

**Agriculture, tillage, grazing [MED]**
- *Messor barbarus* in Spanish dryland cereals: **"Tillage can disturb or even kill ant colonies"**. No-till fields had evenly spaced nests, tilled fields random ones (4 tilled vs 3 no-till fields) — [Univ. Lleida, recercat](https://recercat.cat/handle/10459.1/64721).
- Across 34 no-till and 6 minimally tilled fields, years since conversion explained **12.6%** of nest-density variation. Nest density **peaked at 11–12 years of no-till** and then declined — [recercat](https://recercat.cat/handle/10459.1/58623).
- Yield loss to M. barbarus averaged **0.2% at sowing and 0.6% at harvest (max 9.2%)**, rising with nest density, nest size and years of no-till. This is why farmers may see Messor as a pest — [recercat](https://recercat.cat/handle/10459.1/58631?show=full).
- *P. barbatus* site: cattle grazed until 1993–94. No trampling-mortality figure was given — [Sundaram et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10316491).

**Fire [NA]**
- *P. rugosus* after experimental fire (New Mexico): no significant change in foraging except **more fire-killed insects collected**. **One year later, all experimental colonies were alive and normal** — [Zimmer & Parmenter 1998 (LTER)](https://lternet.edu/biblio/harvester-ants-and-fire-in-a-desert-grassland-ecological-responses-of-pogonomyrmex-rugosus-hymenoptera-formicidae-to-experimental-wildfires-in-central-new-mexico/).
- Great Basin, 358 plots burned 1 to 31+ years earlier: fire **didn't change harvester-ant occupancy but increased nest density** — [USGS, Holbrook et al. 2016](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70175980).
- *P. occidentalis* mounds mostly sit in vegetation-free clearings and **don't burn** (Cole 1932) — [Wikipedia, P. occidentalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pogonomyrmex_occidentalis).

### Inferences
- **Flooding in the cross-section (inference):** heavy rain → water runs down the entrance shafts → top chambers flood first. Workers build a soil rim (levee) around entrances. Brood and queen are carried to drier chambers; whether that is up or down depends on the water table. In waterlogged clay the deep chambers flood from below and brood moves *up*. Seeds in wet granaries are hauled out to dry once the sun returns. Afterwards, foraging dips for hours while workers repair collapsed galleries. An incipient colony with one shallow chamber has nowhere to go, so this is high risk at that stage.
- **Heat:** summer midday shuts the surface. Foraging shifts to dawn, dusk and night, and in mid-summer the colony may aestivate. Brood moves deeper by day and shallower to warm up in spring mornings (the Pogonomyrmex behaviour applied to Messor; an inference).
- **Frost/winter:** the colony retreats to deep chambers and lives off granary stock. Brood production stops before winter.
- **Ploughing:** a sudden event that shears off the upper ~20–30 cm of nest (plough depth is my assumption, not sourced). Founding chambers and young colonies are destroyed outright, and deep mature nests may survive with the queen. In the evidence, tilled fields have fewer and randomly scattered nests.
- **Fire:** mostly survivable for underground colonies (sourced for Pogonomyrmex). It produces a short "windfall" of dead insects and possibly changes in seed supply.
- **Drought** should be the game's main "slow killer": it lowers seed income, raises crowding pressure from neighbours, cuts alate production and reduces founding success.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of Messor colony responses to flooding, nest collapse or frost.
- No measured nest depth or thermal profile for M. structor s.str.
- No data on livestock trampling mortality for Messor (or Pogonomyrmex) nests.
- No steppe (Ukraine/Russia/Anatolia) data on ploughing effects on M. structor.

## 8. Typical causes and rates of colony mortality by stage (founding queen, incipient colony, mature colony)

### Takeaway
Mortality is concentrated at founding. In harvester ants **≥~99% of founding queens die**: in P. occidentalis 2 of 188 survived July–March, and in P. barbatus only 10–50 of "many hundreds" of new nests reach age 1. Young colonies remain vulnerable to neighbours, nest plugging and rain-driven food supply. Once mature (~5 yr in P. barbatus), colonies have low annual mortality and live **20–30 years**. They die mainly when the single queen dies, or earlier in severe droughts. Only ~25% of mature P. barbatus colonies ever produced a daughter colony.

### Cited Findings
**Founding queens**
- *P. occidentalis* [NA]: **2 of 188 founding queens survived from July to March** (Wiernasz & Cole 1995). Density-independent factors cause **>90% of foundress mortality** (Billick et al. 2001). First-year survival falls as foundress density rises (Cole & Wiernasz 2002). Established colonies' foragers kill and even excavate queens — [Wikipedia, P. occidentalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pogonomyrmex_occidentalis).
- *P. barbatus* [NA]: each year after the mating flight "many hundreds of nests [are] created by newly mated queens… but very few of these, **10–50, survive until the following year**". Mating aggregations hold thousands of reproductives (up to ~200 per colony from 100–200 colonies), and queens disperse ~70 m — [Sundaram et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10316491).
- Harvester founding mortality "may reach **99%**" from predation and desiccation, with no workers to help — [PMC2803755](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2803755). In Pogonomyrmex the highest mortality is at the start of colony life — [SICB abstract (Appleby)](https://sicb.org/?p=24367).
- Founding-chamber details in P. occidentalis: many first eggs die or are unembryonated, larvae may eat eggs, and the first workers (nanitics) are ~2 mm, ~33% smaller than normal workers (Nagel & Rettenmeyer 1973) — [Wikipedia, P. occidentalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pogonomyrmex_occidentalis).
- *Messor semirufus* [MED]: queens in forced groups (usurpation attempts) die more often than solitary queens — [HUJI](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/forced-associations-by-young-queens-of-the-harvester-ant-messor-s/).
- Newly mated queens can acquire entomopathogenic fungi at founding ([UNESP, Atta](https://repositorio.unesp.br/items/0601d544-6aba-44f2-b8be-fb43c2394d9e/full)) [GEN].

**Incipient / young colonies (1–4 yr in P. barbatus)**
- P. barbatus colonies grow fast after founding and begin reproducing at **~5 years**, when the queen is about five. 1–2-yr colonies retreat from neighbours, and 3–4-yr colonies fight them repeatedly — [Sundaram et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10316491).
- **Small, young colonies are especially targeted by nest plugging** (*N. cockerelli*) — [Sundaram et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10316491); [Barton et al. 2002](https://bioone.org/journals/the-american-midland-naturalist/volume-148/issue-2/0003-0031(2002)148[0376:TEOPAC]2.0.CO;2/The-Effects-of-Proximity-and-Colony-Age-on-Interspecific-Interference/10.1674/0003-0031(2002)148[0376:TEOPAC]2.0.CO;2.full).
- *P. occidentalis*: **smaller nests are more likely to die**, and survival rises with distance to the nearest neighbour — [Wikipedia, P. occidentalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pogonomyrmex_occidentalis).

**Mature colonies**
- *P. barbatus* colonies **live 20–30 years (about 30)**, founded by a single queen. **"When the queen dies, and all the workers have died, the colony is dead; the colony does not adopt a new queen."** There's no reproductive senescence. Only **~25% of mature colonies had ever produced offspring colonies** (range 1–8) — [Sundaram et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10316491).
- Survival to next year depends on **colony age and the foraging area available among neighbours**. Under drought, more area is needed per colony, and in 2016 mortality hit young adults (5–10 yr) — [Sundaram et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10316491).
- *P. occidentalis*: queens up to **30 years**, many colonies survive 20 years, colonies up to **20,000 workers**, average **worker lifespan ~6 months** — [Wikipedia, P. occidentalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pogonomyrmex_occidentalis). Cole & Wiernasz's long-term dataset (~3,500 colonies, mortality of ~2,000 analysed) underlies their life table — [ESA 2002 abstract](https://esa.confex.com/esa/2002/techprogram/paper_6610.htm); an extension covers 6,066 colonies 1993–2023 — [UH thesis record](https://uh-ir.tdl.org/items/eb49bc1c-e822-454e-b80c-181531b18c07).
- Sympatric competitors can collapse fast. *Veromessor* (formerly Messor) *mimicus* colonies at the P. barbatus site fell from 70 marked in 1999 to **29 (41%) active in 2003 and 6 (8%) in 2004**. *N. cockerelli* fell from 55 colonies (2000) to 41 (75%) in 2003 and 20 (35%) in 2004, then disappeared 2008–2012 — [Sundaram et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10316491).

**Messor structor specifics**
- Colonies of thousands of workers are common; one source gives up to ~5,000 in the wild. Seed stores are "often 1–2 kg" (citation needed in source). Wikipedia calls colonies "typically polygynous". An **Austrian population (Retz) was unicolonial, with no aggression between nests and no distinct mating flights** (Schlick-Steiner et al.) — [Wikipedia, M. structor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messor_structor). The polygyny claim conflicts with common hobbyist descriptions of single-queen colonies and rests on weak citation, so treat it with caution.

- **Stage-specific mortality guide for tuning (inference from Pogonomyrmex numbers):** founding queen → 1-yr colony: ~1–5% survive (2/188 ≈ 1%; 10–50 of "many hundreds"). Young colonies (1–4 yr): moderate annual mortality, sensitive to rain and neighbours. Mature colonies: low annual mortality, roughly 3–5%/yr implied by a 20–30-yr lifespan (my rough inference, not a measured figure), rising sharply in drought years.
- Because M. structor may be polygynous or unicolonial in some populations (contested), a "queen death = colony death" rule fits monogynous populations. Polygynous populations could survive a single queen's death.

### Gaps
- **No field survival data for Messor founding queens or incipient colonies**, and none for M. structor. All quantitative stage-mortality numbers above are from North American *Pogonomyrmex*.
- No lifespan estimate for Messor colonies in the wild.
- Colony size, queen number and nest depth for M. structor s.str. remain poorly sourced (Wikipedia statements carry "citation needed").
- The Gordon & Kulig (1996, 1998) originals weren't read directly; they were cited through Sundaram et al. 2022 and PMC2803755.
